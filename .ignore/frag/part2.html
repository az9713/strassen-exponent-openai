Definition (secants). p. 56 paper Let $S$ be a finite set of distinct points of an affine plane. A secant is a line that contains at least two points of $S$. The set of secants is $\mathcal L$, and $t_i$ is the number of secants through the point $i\in S$.

The paper starts from an identity for real labels. If $v_i\in\mathbb R$ for $i\in S$ and $\sum_{i\in S} v_i=0$, then

$$\sum_{L\in\mathcal L}\Bigl|\sum_{i\in S\cap L}v_i\Bigr|^2=\sum_{i\in S}(t_i-1)\,|v_i|^2 .$$
Gap fill — the two-line computation, and the Hilbert-space version. filled here Expand each square. The term $v_i^2$ appears once for each secant through $i$, so $t_i$ times. For $i\ne j$ the product $v_iv_j$ appears once as an ordered pair, because exactly one line passes through two distinct points and that line is a secant. Hence the left side is $\sum_i t_i v_i^2+\sum_{i\ne j}v_iv_j$. The second sum is $(\sum_i v_i)^2-\sum_i v_i^2=-\sum_i v_i^2$ by the centering hypothesis. This gives the identity.

The same computation holds for vectors $v_i$ in a real Hilbert space with $\sum_i v_i=0$, with $|\cdot|$ the norm and products replaced by inner products. So in a Hilbert space the relation between "secant sums" and "point norms" is an equality. 15.1 An inequality for secant lines

The objects of this subsection are the following. Readers who arrive here directly need four notions from earlier sections; each is restated in one sentence.

  • A spectral space over $F$ (§3) is a metric space $(X,d)$ in which every nonzero tensor over $F$, with its legs placed at finitely many points ("sites") of $X$, has a real number called its price $H$. Prices do not increase under local linear maps, add under tensor product, and the price of a wire of dimension $q$ between sites $a,b$ is $d(a,b)\log q$.
  • A complete metric space is CAT(0) if every pair $a,b$ has a midpoint $m$ with $d(o,m)^2\le\tfrac12 d(o,a)^2+\tfrac12 d(o,b)^2-\tfrac14 d(a,b)^2$ for every point $o$. Hilbert spaces and metric trees are examples. In such a space geodesics are unique, and every finitely supported probability measure has a unique barycenter, the minimizer of the mean squared distance.
  • The Alexandrov angle $\angle_o(x,y)\in[0,\pi]$ between the geodesics from $o$ to $x$ and from $o$ to $y$ is the limit, as two points move toward $o$ along the two geodesics, of the Euclidean comparison angle at $o$. In a Hilbert space it is the usual angle.
  • The transport bound (3.1) of §3: moving a leg of dimension $q$ from site $x$ to site $y$ changes the price by at most $d(x,y)\log q$.
Definition (weighted configuration and the pull $K_C$). p. 56 paper Let $S\subset F^2$ be finite with $|S|\ge 2$. For each $i\in S$ choose a site $x_i$ in a complete CAT(0) spectral space $X$ over $F$ and a mass $a_i\gt 0$. Two points of $S$ may have the same site. Let $o\in X$ be the minimizer of $\sum_i a_i\,d(o,x_i)^2$ and put $r_i=d(o,x_i)$. For a subset $C\subset S$ define $$K_C=\sup_{y\ne o}\Bigl(\sum_{i\in C}a_i r_i\cos\angle_o(x_i,y)\Bigr)_+ ,$$ where $y$ ranges over $X$, $(\cdot)_+$ is the positive part, and a summand with $r_i=0$ is zero. If $X$ is one point, $K_C=0$. For a secant $L$ write $K_L=K_{S\cap L}$.

The paper gives the following interpretation. Suppose the legs belonging to $C$ have "widths" $a_ir_i$, that is, the logarithm of the dimension of leg $i$ is proportional to $a_ir_i$. Sending these legs from a common site $m$ to their destinations costs $\sum_{i\in C}a_ir_i\,d(m,x_i)$. If $m$ moves away from $o$ toward $y$ at unit speed, this cost initially changes at the rate $-\sum_{i\in C}a_ir_i\cos\angle_o(x_i,y)$ (first variation formula, stated below). So $K_C$ is the largest initial saving per unit distance that the group $C$ gains by moving its common site off $o$; the positive part accounts for the option of staying at $o$.

Gap fill — $K_C$ on a line and in a Hilbert space. filled here Let $X=\mathbb R$. Then $o=\sum_i a_ix_i/\sum_i a_i$, and $\cos\angle_o(x_i,y)=\operatorname{sign}(x_i-o)\operatorname{sign}(y-o)$. Theorem 15.1 (Incidence inequality). p. 56 paper If $F$ is algebraically closed and $\omega_F\lt 9/4$, then for every finite configuration of affine points $S\subset F^2$, weighted sites $(x_i,a_i)_{i\in S}$ in a complete CAT(0) spectral space over $F$, and secants $\mathcal L$ as above, $$\sum_{L\in\mathcal L}K_L^2\ \le\ \sum_{i\in S}(t_i-1)\,a_i^2r_i^2 .\tag{15.1}$$

The proof has five steps. Its hinge is stated first, because the steps are otherwise hard to motivate.

AHA. The points of $S$ are used twice, in two unrelated roles. As points of $F^2$ they are projected to the line $\mathbb P^1$ along a direction that depends on a parameter $s$; two projected points collide exactly when $s$ is the direction of the secant through them. As labels they index the legs of one sphere tensor whose marked points are the projections. A single nonzero rational function of $s$ on $\mathbb P^1$ has orders that sum to zero, and at each collision parameter the order of vanishing equals the excess energy of the fused legs. This degree count is linear in energy. Because the width of a fermionic leg of energy $h$ is proportional to $\sqrt h$, it becomes a bound on a sum of squared widths, which is the form of (15.1).

15.2 The sharp local link inequality

The second statement specializes Theorem 15.1 to the affine plane over a finite field and rewrites it as a lower bound for the energy of an incidence graph. Some background on finite planes is needed first.

Background — finite affine planes, parallel classes, and the linear spectral gap. filled here Let $q$ be a prime power. The affine plane $\mathbb F_q^2$ has $q^2$ points and $q^2+q$ lines. The lines fall into $q+1$ parallel classes (one for each direction, i.e. each point of $\mathbb P^1(\mathbb F_q)$); a class consists of $q$ disjoint lines of $q$ points each. Every point lies on exactly one line of each class, and two lines of different classes meet in exactly one point.

Retain the lines of $s$ classes. The bipartite incidence graph has $q^2$ point vertices of degree $s$, $sq$ line vertices of degree $q$, and $sq^2$ edges. Its normalized incidence operator $M$ sends a function $f$ on points to the function $(Mf)(L)=(sq)^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in L}f(i)$ on retained lines. The space of functions on points decomposes orthogonally as the constants, plus, for each of the $q+1$ classes $\mathcal C$, the $(q-1)$-dimensional space $W_{\mathcal C}$ of mean-zero functions that are constant on the lines of $\mathcal C$ (dimension check: $1+(q+1)(q-1)=q^2$). For $f\in W_{\mathcal C}$ and a line $L'$ of another class, $\sum_{i\in L'}f(i)=0$ because $L'$ meets each line of $\mathcal C$ once. Hence $M^*Mf=\tfrac1s f$ if $\mathcal C$ is retained and $Mf=0$ otherwise. The singular values of $M$ are therefore $1$ (constants), $s^{-1/2}$ (multiplicity $s(q-1)$) and $0$.

Consequence for Hilbert-space placements. Put the point vertices at vectors $x_i$ with mean $o$ and the line vertices at vectors $y_L$. With $A=q^{-2}\sum_i\|x_i-o\|^2$ and $B=(sq)^{-1}\sum_L\|y_L-o\|^2$, the mean over edges of $\langle x_i-o,y_L-o\rangle$ is at most $\sqrt{AB/s}$ in absolute value (apply the singular value bound $s^{-1/2}$ coordinatewise to the centered function $x-o$). The mean squared edge length is therefore at least $A+B-2\sqrt{AB/s}$. Corollary 15.2 states that, under the hypothesis, the same bound with the same constant holds for placements in CAT(0) spectral spaces, where no linear operator is available.
Definition (the quantities $e$ and $V$). p. 58 paper Suppose $F\supseteq\mathbb F_q$. Take the $q^2$ points of $\mathbb F_q^2$ and the lines of $s$ parallel classes, $1\le s\le q+1$. A placement assigns to each point $i$ a site $x_i\in X$ and to each retained line $L$ a site $y_L\in X$. The number $e$ is the mean of $d(x_i,y_L)^2$ over the $sq^2$ incidences $i\in L$. The inequality. Let $o$ be the barycenter of the point sites alone, with unit masses. Write $r_i=d(o,x_i)$ and $r_L=d(o,y_L)$, and

$$A=q^{-2}\sum_ir_i^2,\qquad B=(sq)^{-1}\sum_{L\ \mathrm{retained}}r_L^2 .$$

In a CAT(0) space the Alexandrov angle at $o$ is at most the Euclidean comparison angle, so the law of cosines holds as an inequality ("triangle comparison"):

$$d(x_i,y_L)^2\ \ge\ r_i^2+r_L^2-2r_ir_L\cos\angle_o(x_i,y_L),$$

with the cross term read as zero when $r_i=0$ or $r_L=0$. Sum over the $sq^2$ incidences. Background — stable sorting, and what a network for an indicator does. filled here A sorting procedure is stable if records with equal keys leave in the order in which they entered. 16.1 Evaluating a table by sorting its queries

A station of arity $t$ is a vertex of a network that carries an arbitrary table $f:\{0,1\}^t\to K$. A trivalent network that evaluates one such table on one query can need a number of gates proportional to $2^t$, because the table has $2^t$ independent entries. The lemma shows that $2^t$ queries to the same table can be answered together for $O((1+t)^p)$ gates per query.

Lemma 16.2 (Batched table evaluation). p. 59 paper Assume the ordinary-width stable-sort bound of Theorem 16.1 over $k$, with exponent $p\gt1$. Let a network of binary wires and $K$-valued tables represent a tensor $T$, where $K/k$ is any field extension. If $t_v\le u$ is the arity of station $v$ and $s=\sum_v(1+t_v)$, then $T^{\otimes2^u}$ has an exact trivalent $K$-network of size $$O\bigl(2^u\,s\,(1+u)^{p-1}\bigr).$$ The constant is independent of $K$ and of the entries in the tables.
AHA. The product $\prod_if(x_i)$ over a batch of queries depends only on how many times each address $z$ occurs: it equals $\prod_zf(z)^{c_z}$. Sorting the queries makes the counts $c_z$ readable by a single pass, and the power $f(z)^{c_z}$ is then produced by $t+1$ fixed one-bit factors $f(z)^{2^j}$ attached to the binary digits of $c_z$. The table entries enter only as fixed coefficients at fixed positions, so no gate has to "look up" anything.

One table, one batch. Let $f:\{0,1\}^t\to K$ with $t\ge1$, and let the $2^t$ query addresses $x_1,\dots,x_{2^t}\in\{0,1\}^t$ be exterior legs. Let $c_z$ be the number of $i$ with $x_i=z$. The required value is $\prod_if(x_i)=\prod_zf(z)^{c_z}$, with $f(z)^0=1$ also when $f(z)=0$.

Create one dictionary row for each address $z$, with a count field of $t+1$ bits whose value is summed over (a "guess"). The count field has $t+1$ bits because $0\le c_z\le2^t$. On bit $j$ of the count field of row $z$ ($0\le j\le t$) place a one-leg table with entries $(1,\ f(z)^{2^j})$: value $1$ if the bit is $0$ and $f(z)^{2^j}$ if it is $1$. The row position determines $z$, so these two entries are constants of the network. If the count field holds the number $c=\sum_j\beta_j2^j$, the product of the $t+1$ factors is $f(z)^c$.

Create one query row for each query, holding its address and zeros in the count field. Sort the $2^{t+1}$ rows stably by address and, within an address, by row type, so that the dictionary row of $z$ follows all queries to $z$. Run a counter along the sorted list: it starts at zero, increases by one at each query row, and at a dictionary row it is required to equal the carried count and is then reset to zero.

Exactness. Fix the exposed addresses. The sorted list is unique because the sort is stable. The counter states are then forced, and at the dictionary row of $z$ the counter holds $c_z$, so the only count field that passes the check is $c_z$, including $c_z=0$ at an address that receives no query. So exactly one setting of the internal wires has a nonzero contribution, and its contribution is $\prod_zf(z)^{c_z}$. No multiplicity occurs, so the identity holds in every characteristic and also when some table entries are zero.

Size of one batch. The counter and the equality checks are ripple-carry operations on $(t+1)$-bit numbers at $2^{t+1}$ rows: $O(2^t(1+t))$ gates. The rows have $O(t+1)$ bits and there are $2^{t+1}$ of them, so part 1 of Theorem 16.1 (with $h$ replaced by $t+1$) gives a sorting network of size $O(2^t(1+t)^p)$. That network has tables with entries in $k$ and represents a $0/1$ tensor; read over $K\supseteq k$ it represents the same tensor.

All stations, all copies. Take $2^u$ copies of the original network. For each station $v$ of arity $t=t_v\ge1$, group its $2^u$ copies into $2^{u-t}$ batches of $2^t$ and replace each batch by the gadget above; a station of arity zero is a scalar. Connect the exposed address bits as the wires of the original network prescribe, separately in each copy. Summing over the wires of all copies gives $T^{\otimes2^u}$. The size is at most a constant times

$$\begin{aligned}\sum_v2^{u-t_v}\cdot2^{t_v}(1+t_v)^p&=2^u\sum_v(1+t_v)^p\\ &\le\ 2^u(1+u)^{p-1}\sum_v(1+t_v)=2^u\,s\,(1+u)^{p-1}.\end{aligned}$$ ∎

Per copy of $T$, the bound is $s\,(1+u)^{p-1}$: each unit of $s$ (a station or a wire end) is charged $(1+u)^{p-1}$. This is the form in which §27 uses the lemma, with $2^u$ as part of the replication factor $d$ of Theorem 2.1.

Background — ultralimits of metric spaces, and why they make constants non-effective. filled here An ultrafilter $\mathcal U$ on an index set $\Lambda$ is a finitely additive $\{0,1\}$-valued measure on all subsets of $\Lambda$. Every bounded family of real numbers $(c_\lambda)$ has a limit $\lim_{\mathcal U}c_\lambda$, the unique number $c$ such that $\{\lambda:|c_\lambda-c|\lt\varepsilon\}$ has measure $1$ for every $\varepsilon\gt0$. Given pointed metric spaces $(X_\lambda,d_\lambda,*_\lambda)$, the pointed ultralimit consists of the families $(x_\lambda)$ with $d_\lambda(x_\lambda,*_\lambda)$ bounded, with distance $\lim_{\mathcal U}d_\lambda(x_\lambda,y_\lambda)$, modulo distance zero. The barycenter of finitely many weighted points of the ultralimit is the limit of the coordinate barycenters $b_\lambda$: the variance inequality $\sum_i\mu_id_\lambda(z_\lambda,x_{i,\lambda})^2\ge\sum_i\mu_id_\lambda(b_\lambda,x_{i,\lambda})^2+d_\lambda(z_\lambda,b_\lambda)^2$ passes to the limit and shows that $\lim_{\mathcal U}b_\lambda$ is the unique minimizer.

Non-principal ultrafilters exist by the axiom of choice and cannot be written down. A proof that derives a contradiction from an ultralimit of hypothetical counterexamples shows that a constant exists and gives no bound for it. This is the source of the non-effective constants in Lemma 17.3.

Background — rearrangeable (Beneš) networks as a three-factor decomposition. filled here Beneš's theorem [9], in the form used here: every permutation $\sigma$ of a product $\mathsf A\times\mathsf B$ can be written $\sigma=\sigma_3\sigma_2\sigma_1$, where $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_3$ change only the $\mathsf A$-coordinate and $\sigma_2$ changes only the $\mathsf B$-coordinate.

Proof. Let $a=|\mathsf A|$. Form a bipartite multigraph with a left copy and a right copy of $\mathsf B$ and one edge for every element $(\alpha,\beta)$: if $\sigma(\alpha,\beta)=(\alpha',\beta')$ the edge joins left $\beta$ to right $\beta'$. Every vertex has degree $a$. By König's edge-coloring theorem an $a$-regular bipartite multigraph has a proper coloring of its edges with $a$ colors, which we take to be the elements of $\mathsf A$; at each vertex every color occurs once. Let $\gamma(\alpha,\beta)$ be the color of the edge of $(\alpha,\beta)$. Define $\sigma_1(\alpha,\beta)=(\gamma,\beta)$; it is a bijection because the colors at the left vertex $\beta$ are distinct. Define $\sigma_2(\gamma,\beta)=(\gamma,\beta')$; it is a bijection because the edges of one color form a perfect matching. Define $\sigma_3(\gamma,\beta')=(\alpha',\beta')$; it is a bijection because the colors at the right vertex $\beta'$ are distinct. $\square$

The middle factor is a family of permutations of $\mathsf B$, one for each color, and can be decomposed again if $\mathsf B$ is itself a product. For a product of $\ell+1$ coordinates this gives $2\ell+1$ factors, each changing a single coordinate. With two-element coordinates this is the classical Beneš network of $2\log_2n-1$ switching stages.

The $K_V$ generate $G$. paper Treat $[c]\times U^m$ as one coordinate, next to the $\ell$ coordinates of $U^\ell$. Applying the decomposition repeatedly writes every element of $G$ as a product of a bounded number of stages (at most $2\ell+1$ by the count in the box), each changing one coordinate. A stage that changes the $a$th coordinate of $U^\ell$ lies in $K_{L_a}$ for the coordinate line $L_a\subset\mathbb F_q^\ell$; a stage that changes the auxiliary coordinate lies in $K_0$. The paper spells out the factorization because the routing protocol of §18 uses its factors as the designated path from $i$ to $\pi(i)$.

The stages are in $G$: computing the coloring. paper Membership in $G$ requires a program with $nh^{O(1)}$ word operations, so the edge coloring must be computed in that time. The paper gives the following procedure (see also [21]).

  • Even degree. An even-regular bipartite multigraph splits into two regular halves by alternating two colors around Euler tours. (Every closed walk in a bipartite graph has even length, so the alternation closes up, and at each vertex half of the edges receive each color.)
  • Odd degree $a$. First remove one perfect matching, found as follows. Let $v$ be the number of vertices on each side and $H$ the least integer with $2^H\gt av$. Multiply every edge multiplicity by $\lfloor2^H/a\rfloor$ and add copies of an arbitrary "dummy" perfect matching to reach degree exactly $2^H$. The number of dummy copies per vertex is $2^H-a\lfloor2^H/a\rfloor\lt a$, so the total dummy multiplicity is less than $av\lt2^H$. Split into two regular halves $H$ times, each time keeping the half with fewer dummy edges. The dummy count at least halves in each step, so after $H$ steps it is less than $1$, that is, zero; the degree is then $2^H/2^H=1$. The kept graph is a perfect matching made of original edges. In each split, an edge of multiplicity $\mu$ contributes $\lfloor\mu/2\rfloor$ copies to each half and only the odd leftover copies are distributed by Euler tours; so it suffices to store the original edges and the dummy matching with their multiplicities.
  • After the matching is removed the degree is even and is halved. The recursion has $O(\log a)$ levels.

The total time is linear in the number of edges up to logarithmic factors. Here the number of edges is $|\mathcal I|\lt2n$.

17.3 Uniform displacement

If $G$ acts by isometries on a metric space and every hop moves a point $x$ by at most $1$, the triangle inequality along the coordinate factorization gives $d(x,\pi x)\le2\ell+1$, a bound that grows with $\ell$. The next lemma replaces it by an absolute constant, by finding a point fixed by all of $G$.

Background — three facts about complete CAT(0) spaces $Y$. filled here (i) Variance inequality. If $b$ is the barycenter of a finitely supported probability measure $\mu$, then $\int d(z,y)^2\,d\mu(y)\ge\int d(b,y)^2\,d\mu(y)+d(z,b)^2$ for every $z$. (ii) Circumcenter. A bounded set $\Omega$ has a unique smallest enclosing closed ball; its center is the circumcenter of $\Omega$. If $\Omega\subset\bar B(p,\varrho)$ and $p\in\Omega$, the circumradius is at most $\varrho$, so the circumcenter is within $\varrho$ of $p$. (iii) Equivariance. Barycenters, circumcenters, midpoints and geodesics are defined by the metric alone. An isometry that preserves a measure or a set fixes its barycenter or circumcenter; in particular a group that has a bounded orbit fixes the circumcenter of that orbit (the Bruhat–Tits fixed point theorem), and the midpoint of two fixed points is fixed.
Background — Garland's local-to-global method and its metric version. filled here Let a two-dimensional simplicial complex be given and a map $y$ from its vertices to a space $Y$. The energy of $y$ is the mean of $d(y_{W_1},y_{W_2})^2$ over edges. Every edge $W_1W_2$ of a triangle is opposite to the third vertex $V$ of that triangle, and belongs to the link of $V$ (the graph formed by the edges opposite to $V$ in the triangles through $V$). Averaging over triangles and their vertices therefore writes the energy as an average over vertices $V$ of the energies of the links. Suppose that for every $V$ there is a point $z_V$ whose mean squared distance to the link vertices is at most $\kappa$ times the link energy, with a fixed $\kappa\lt1$. Moving every $y_V$ halfway toward $z_V$ multiplies the total energy by at most $\kappa$. Iteration gives a limit map of energy zero, i.e. a constant map.

For a real Hilbert space $Y$ and $z_V$ the mean of the link, $\kappa=1/(2\lambda_1)$ where $\lambda_1$ is the first positive eigenvalue of the normalized Laplacian of the link, and the condition is $\lambda_1\gt\tfrac12$. Garland [29] used this to prove vanishing of cohomology for groups acting on Bruhat–Tits buildings; Izeki and Nayatani [30] proved fixed-point theorems for isometric actions on CAT(0) spaces under a nonlinear version of the link condition. The paper's Lemma 17.2 uses the same scheme on a small complex (one triangle for each flag $0\subset L\subset P$), with one change: the map $y$ is required to send each vertex $V$ to a point fixed by its subgroup $K_V$, and each $z_V$ must also be fixed by $K_V$. The limit point is then fixed by every $K_V$.
Lemma 17.2. p. 63 paper Suppose $G$ acts isometrically on a complete CAT(0) space $Y$ in which the inequalities of Lemma 17.1 hold for all placements. There is an absolute constant $C_0$ such that, if $d(x,gx)\le1$ for all $g\in\bigcup_VK_V$, then $G$ fixes a point within $C_0/2$ of $x$. In particular $d(x,\pi x)\le C_0$.

The aim is to find, for each $V$, another point $z_V$ fixed by $K_V$ such that, for a uniform flag through $V$ with opposite vertices $W_1,W_2$ and $W$ uniform in $\{W_1,W_2\}$,

$$\mathbb E\,d(z_V,y_W)^2\ \le\ \tfrac34\,\mathbb E\,d(y_{W_1},y_{W_2})^2 .\tag{17.2}$$

The vertex $V=0$. The opposite edges are the pairs $(L,P)$ with $L\subset P$, i.e. the incidences of $\mathrm{PG}(\ell-1,q)$. Let $z_0$ be the barycenter of the points $y_L$ (uniform over lines $L$). The vertex $V=L$. The opposite edges join $0$ to the planes $P\supset L$. Let $z_L$ be the barycenter of the $y_P$ with $P\supset L$; it is fixed by $K_L$ because $K_L\subset K_P$. The variance inequality with test point $y_0$ gives $\mathbb E_Pd(y_0,y_P)^2\ge\mathbb E_Pd(z_L,y_P)^2+d(y_0,z_L)^2$. The left side is the link energy and the right side is $2\,\mathbb E\,d(z_L,y_W)^2$. So (17.2) holds with coefficient $\tfrac12$.

The vertex $V=P$: where the difficulty is. The opposite edges join $0$ to the lines $L\subset P$; the link energy is $e_P=\mathbb E_{L\subset P}\,d(y_0,y_L)^2$. The barycenter construction fails here for a reason of symmetry, not of expansion: $K_P$ is the largest of the three groups, and the points $y_0$, $y_L$ are fixed only by the smaller groups $K_0$, $K_L$, so their barycenter need not be fixed by $K_P$. The paper replaces the link by a large affine incidence graph made of $K_P$-translates of $y_0$ and the $y_L$, whose point barycenter is moved by $K_P$ only a small distance.

17.4 Finite tests on permutation orbits

Lemma 17.2 is about an action on a metric space. The routing protocol of §18 has no such space; it has a finite address set and a table of proposed prices. This subsection defines a local test on such a table and proves that passing the test almost everywhere implies the conclusion of Lemma 17.2 for the mean payload price.

Definition (core generators, commodities, tests). p. 65 paper
  • Core generators: a finite symmetric subset of $\bigcup_VK_V$ that contains the factors of a fixed coordinate factorization of $\pi$ and their inverses. For each input they define an undirected graph on $\mathcal I$ of bounded degree, with a core link from each label to its image under each generator. Each $i$ has a path to $\pi(i)$ along the factors, of length at most a fixed rational number $a_{\mathrm p}$ independent of $h$ and of the input.
  • Test parameters: the core generators, a ball radius, a maximum number of sites, and a maximum local dimension.
  • Commodities: each payload wire (the one-bit wire from $i$ to $\pi(i)$) is a commodity with its own index; so is every nonzero $k$-tensor of permitted format on an ordered list of distinct sites inside a common test ball, in fixed coordinate bases. The index is global: two balls that contain the same tensor on the same ordered sites refer to one index. There are $O(|\mathcal I|)$ indices.
  • Price array: a nonnegative number for each commodity. The number assigned to the payload wire $i\to\pi(i)$ is written $x_{i,\pi(i)}$. A payload price is capped at $a_{\mathrm p}$; each other format has a uniform rational cap that suffices to create the tensor at one site and transport its legs in binary along core links of the ball.
  • Test on a ball: compare the array with an ordinary spectral price on the sites of the ball. The error is the largest of: the absolute differences between assigned and spectral prices of the commodities of the ball, and the positive excesses above $1$ of the spectral lengths of the core links inside the ball. The discrepancy is the minimum error over all ordinary prices, the collapsed price included. The center passes to tolerance $\rho$ if the discrepancy is at most $\rho$.

The minimum is attained: a price with bounded error gives every core link length at most $1$ plus the error; the core links connect the ball, so by the spanning-tree bound of §16.2 the scale is bounded, and prices of bounded scale form a compact set.

Lemma 17.3 (Finite detection). p. 65 paper For each $\ell$ there are finite test parameters and $\rho\gt0$ such that the following holds for all sufficiently large $h$ and every valid permutation input. If all but a fraction $\rho$ of the centers pass to tolerance $\rho$, the assigned payload prices satisfy $$|\mathcal I|^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal I}x_{i,\pi(i)}\ \le\ C_0+1 .$$ Here $C_0$ is the absolute constant of Lemma 17.2.

The number $\rho$, the radius and the format bounds are obtained by contradiction from an ultralimit. The proof gives no estimate for them, and they depend on $\ell$.

The sequence of counterexamples. Suppose the lemma fails for some $\ell$. Then for every choice of finite test parameters and of $\rho$ there are counterexamples at arbitrarily large $h$. Choose a sequence indexed by $\nu=1,2,\dots$ in which the sets of core generators increase and exhaust the countable set $\bigcup_VK_V$, the radii and format bounds tend to infinity, the tolerance and the permitted exceptional fraction tend to zero, $h_\nu\to\infty$, and the factorization of $\pi$ and the cap $a_{\mathrm p}$ are kept fixed. In each counterexample the mean payload price exceeds $C_0+1$. The aim is an isometric action of $G$ in which every hop displaces a point $x$ by at most $1$ and $\pi$ displaces it by at least $C_0+1$, contradicting Lemma 17.2.

Charts and marks. At each passing center $i$ take a complete CAT(0) spectral realization $X_i$ of a price that attains the test on the ball of $i$ (the chart at $i$); at a failing center take the one-point space. For $g\in G$ the mark of $g$ in the chart at $i$ is the site $g^{-1}i$ (the center itself if that site is not in the ball). Since $g^{-1}$ is a fixed word in elements of the $K_V$, for large $\nu$ these elements are core generators and the ball contains the whole path from $i$ to $g^{-1}i$; each link of the path has length at most $1$ plus the tolerance, so the mark is at a distance from the center that is bounded for each fixed $g$.

The limit space. For each $\nu$ form the product of the charts over all centers $i\in\mathcal I$ with the root-mean-square distance $\bigl(|\mathcal I|^{-1}\sum_id_i(\cdot,\cdot)^2\bigr)^{1/2}$, pointed at the vector of centers. Take a pointed ultralimit over $\nu$, and let $Y$ be the closed convex hull of the limits $\mathbf m_g$ of the vectors of marks, $g\in G$. Products with the root-mean-square distance and ultralimits of complete CAT(0) spaces are complete CAT(0) spaces (the midpoint inequality is preserved by averaging and by limits). Both inequalities of Lemma 17.1 hold in $Y$: they hold in each chart; in a product the barycenter is taken coordinatewise and $e$, $A$, $B$ are the averages of their coordinate values; in the ultralimit the barycenter is the limit of the barycenters (background box in §17.1); and a closed convex subset contains the barycenters of the distributions supported in it. Lemma 17.1 is stated for spectral spaces over $k$; this step transfers its two inequalities to $Y$ and not a spectral structure, which is what the hypothesis of Lemma 17.2 asks for ("in which the inequalities of Lemma 17.1 hold for all placements").

The action. For $u\in G$ the paper claims that $\mathbf m_g\mapsto\mathbf m_{ug}$ extends to an isometry of $Y$. A dense subset of $Y$ consists of the points given by finite expressions of geodesic interpolations of marks. Fix two such expressions. In the chart at $i$, the translated expressions involve the labels $(ug)^{-1}i=g^{-1}(u^{-1}i)$; these are the labels of the original expressions in the chart at $u^{-1}i$. So the comparison is between two realizations, in two different charts, of prices on the same finite list of labels. For large $\nu$ both balls contain the list, and both centers pass except for a fraction tending to zero (the map $i\mapsto u^{-1}i$ preserves the uniform measure). Any fixed tensor on the distinct labels of the list is a commodity with one global index, and both realizations price it within the tolerance of its assigned price; so the two realizations differ by at most twice the tolerance on that tensor once the format bounds include it. The paper concludes by a compactness argument that the distance between the two expressions in the chart at $i$ and in the chart at $u^{-1}i$ differ by a quantity that tends to zero uniformly: a violating sequence would have a subsequence with a fixed pattern of coincidences among the labels; the word lengths bound the diameters, hence the scales (spanning-tree bound); limiting prices on the list exist in both charts and agree on every tensor; and by Convention 3 of §16.2 the limiting distances between the expressions agree. On exceptional pairs the distances are bounded (word lengths in a passing chart, zero in a collapsed chart), so they contribute a vanishing amount to the mean square. Reindexing the average by $i\mapsto u^{-1}i$ gives $d(u\mathsf E_1,u\mathsf E_2)=d(\mathsf E_1,\mathsf E_2)$ in the limit for all pairs of expressions. The map therefore is well defined and isometric on a dense subset, extends to $Y$, has the inverse coming from $u^{-1}$, and satisfies the group law.

The contradiction. Let $x=\mathbf m_{\mathrm{id}}$, the limit of the vectors of centers. For $g\in K_V$, the hop $g^{-1}$ is eventually a core generator; in a passing chart the mark $g^{-1}i$ is at distance at most $1$ plus the tolerance from $i$, and in a failing chart at distance $0$; so $d(x,gx)\le1$. The mark of $\pi^{-1}$ in the chart at $i$ is the site $\pi(i)$. In a passing chart its distance to the center is the spectral price of the payload wire $i\to\pi(i)$, which agrees with the assigned price $x_{i,\pi(i)}$ up to the tolerance; in a failing chart the distance is $0$ and the assigned price is at most $a_{\mathrm p}$, and such charts have vanishing measure. So $d(x,\pi^{-1}x)$ is the ultralimit of the root mean square of the assigned payload prices, which is at least their arithmetic mean, which exceeds $C_0+1$. Since $d(x,\pi x)=d(\pi^{-1}x,x)$, this contradicts $d(x,\pi x)\le C_0$ from Lemma 17.2. ∎

18.1 From a failed price test to a routing identity

Fix $\ell$, the core generators and the test parameters of Lemma 17.3, and write $M=|\mathcal I|$, so $n\le M\lt2n$. Sending one bit along the designated path from $i$ to $\pi(i)$ uses up to $a_{\mathrm p}$ core links, and $a_{\mathrm p}$ grows with $\ell$. The proposition replaces this by a sum of networks in which the mean number of link uses per payload bit is bounded by an absolute constant.

Proposition 18.1. p. 67 paper Suppose $\omega_k\lt9/4$ and fix $H\ge1$. For all sufficiently large $h$, every valid set of tags and every $h\le w\le h^H$, the associated permutation tensor for $w$-bit payloads is an exact sum over $k$ of networks with the following properties.
  1. Each label has a bounded number of fixed slots per core generator. A used slot carries $O(w)$ bits to a neighbor along the generator or its inverse. Both ends belong to the same prescribed group of at most $2^{th+O(1)}$ labels. The sum of the carried widths in any summand is at most $Cnw$, where $C$ is independent of $\ell$ and $t$.
  2. At each label a fixed trivalent network of size $O(w(1+\log w)^{O(1)})$ implements the local maps. Its configuration consists of a bounded list of menu indices, widths and offsets.
  3. A summand is specified by $O(n\log(2w))$ bits. Given these bits and the tags, a deterministic controller computes the local configurations and matchings in $nh^{O(1)}$ word operations. The exponents and slot capacities are independent of $H$. The running time may have a multiplicative constant depending on $H$.

Here $t=(m+2)/(m+\ell)$ is the number of (17.1), and the "permutation tensor" for valid tags is the tensor of the $M$ bundles of $w$ parallel one-bit wires from $i$ to $\pi(i)$. The proof gives $C=2(C_0+4)$, which is $208$ for $C_0=100$.

Choose a dyadic capacity $U=2^{th+O(1)}$ at least twice the largest group (a new meaning of $U$).   3. First assignment. For each list install enough candidate batches for all its chunks. One assignment graph places the source ends of a batch in $K$ slots and another places the target ends.
  4. Second assignment. Whole batches are assigned to a shared pool of $\lceil6m_*n/K\rceil$ physical modules, each with $K$ input and $K$ output positions. At most $3m_*n/K$ batches are active, which is at most half the pool, and the number of candidate batches is a bounded multiple of the pool size; so the assignment-graph property applies again, and the two lists of an active batch move together to its module. Counting idle modules, at most $\lceil6m_*n/K\rceil K\le7m_*n$ positions are installed for large $h$.
  5. Modules. Every module is one copy of $\operatorname{Tag}(h',w')$ with $h'=\log_2K=th+O(1)$. Unmatched positions are filled in order, unused bits are pinned to zero, and an idle module receives identity tags and zero payloads. All assignments are deterministic functions of the term and its tags. Because the assignment graphs have bounded degree, the switches cost $O(nw)$ in total.

Control. The remaining control data are tags, selectors and slot descriptions. The controller's computation is verified inside the network by guessing a time-stamped execution, checking each instruction, and sorting all memory transactions by address, time and access order with a comparison network, so that every read can be checked against the last write. Fixed initial memory and padding make the accepting execution unique for each legal selector, which keeps the sum exact. A comparison network also checks that the original tags are distinct. For fixed $H$ and $h\le w\le h^H$ the size of everything outside the shorter calls is

$$D_{\mathrm{control}}(h,w)=O_H\bigl(nw(\log(2h))^{O(1)}+nh^J\bigr),\qquad J\gt2 .\tag{18.1}$$

The first term pays for the local maps of Proposition 18.1(2); the second for $nh^{O(1)}$ word operations, their bit implementation and the memory check. Here $J$ is an exponent (a new meaning of $J$), and the exponents do not depend on $H$. The paper states these claims without further proof. With $7m_*n/K=7m_*n/2^{h'}$ modules the result is the recurrence

$$S(h,w)\ \le\ D_{\mathrm{control}}(h,w)+\frac{7m_*n}{2^{h'}}\,S(h',w'),\qquad h'=th+O(1),\quad w'=\lceil Yw\rceil .$$

Wide payloads

Background — Batcher's sorting networks. filled here A comparator network is a fixed sequence of compare-and-exchange operations on pairs of positions; the sequence does not depend on the data. A sequence is bitonic if it increases and then decreases, or is a cyclic shift of such a sequence. For a bitonic sequence of even length $\mathsf N$, comparing position $i$ with position $i+\mathsf N/2$ for all $i\lt\mathsf N/2$ produces two bitonic halves such that every element of the first is at most every element of the second (it suffices to check this for $0/1$ sequences, by the zero–one principle). Recursion sorts a bitonic sequence with $(\mathsf N/2)\log_2\mathsf N$ comparators. Sorting two halves recursively, reversing one, and merging gives a count $\mathsf B(\mathsf N)=2\mathsf B(\mathsf N/2)+(\mathsf N/2)\log_2\mathsf N$, hence $\mathsf B(\mathsf N)=\tfrac14\mathsf N\log_2\mathsf N\,(\log_2\mathsf N+1)$ for $\mathsf N$ a power of two (Batcher [8]; check: $\mathsf B(2)=1$, $\mathsf B(4)=6$). With $\mathsf N=n=2^h$ this is $O(nh^2)$ comparators. A comparator on records with $h$-bit keys and $w$-bit payloads is a Boolean circuit of $O(h+w)$ gates.

The factor $Y^{-b}$ is the reason for the gradual exponent $\mathsf u$. A call has width $w'\approx Yw$ with $Y\lt1$, so its width exponent $a'$ can be smaller than $a$, and near the threshold where the cheap exponent $\zeta$ starts the call may fall into the range of a larger exponent. From tagged permutations to stable sorting

paper For $2^s$ records of $v$ bits, a stable sort uses one $\operatorname{Tag}(s,v)$ and $O(2^s(s+v))$ additional gates: give each proposed output record a guessed position in the input list; use the tagged permutation to check that this assignment sends every output to an equal input; and check along the output list that the pairs (key, guessed input position) are strictly increasing in lexicographic order.

Gap fill — why these checks force the stably sorted output. filled here The tagged permutation check says that the guessed positions $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_{2^s}$ are distinct and that output $j$ equals input $\gamma_j$; in particular they have the same key. So $\gamma$ is a bijection from output positions to input positions that preserves keys, and it maps the outputs with key $\kappa$ bijectively onto the inputs with key $\kappa$. The order check says that keys do not decrease along the output and that, among outputs with equal key, $\gamma$ increases. An increasing bijection between two finite ordered sets is unique. So $\gamma$ is determined by the input, also when records are identical, and the output is the stable sort. Exactly one guess is accepted, so the network is exact.

The stable-sort tensor therefore inherits (18.3). This completes part 2 of Theorem 16.1.

Packing ordinary-width records

For records of width $O(h)$ the bound (18.3) does not apply, since it needs width at least $h^A$. The paper applies (18.3) at two scales in each pass of a radix sort, with two choices of $\zeta$ (the letters $a$, $B$, $s$, $P$ below have the meanings of this paragraph only):

  • $\zeta=\tfrac12$, with threshold exponent $a\gt1$;
  • $\zeta=1/(2a)$, with threshold exponent $B\gt1$.

Start with $O(n)$ records whose keys and payloads have $O(h)$ bits. The radix digit has $s=\lfloor h^{1/a}\rfloor$ bits. Run $O(h/s)$ stable passes, least significant digit first, the last digit padded with zeros.

One pass.

  1. Pad the list to blocks of $2^{2s}$ slots.
  2. Local sort. Stably sort each block by (dummy flag, digit), dummies last in their old order.
  3. Packs. Choose a power of two $P\in[h^{B-1},2h^{B-1})$. Extend each run of one real digit to a multiple of $P$ by dummies at its end. Then each pack of $P$ consecutive slots contains records of at most one real digit. A fixed allowance of $O((2^s+1)P)$ extra slots per block covers the padding of all runs (at most $2^s$ digit values, less than $P$ dummies each), keeps the old dummies and rounds the length to a multiple of $P$. Scans compute the run lengths and the destinations of all old records; a second local tagged permutation checks these destinations; new dummies are appended with guessed destinations that are required to increase strictly, so that exactly one filling is valid.
  4. Global sort. Scan each pack for its digit and stably sort the packs by digit, empty packs last. Records with equal digits keep their order inside a block and across blocks.

From the radix sort to $\operatorname{Tag}(h,h)$. paper Take the union of the $n$ source records and the $n$ proposed target records. Give each source its claimed destination and each target its prescribed position, and radix-sort the union by destination, sources before targets at equal destination. A scan over the real records, with a register of $O(h)$ bits, requires for each destination in order exactly one source followed by one target with equal payloads. This rejects repeated or missing tags and verifies the payloads. Thus $\operatorname{Tag}(h,h)$ has an exact trivalent network of size $O(nh^p)$ with

$$p=2-\frac1{2a},\qquad1\lt p\lt2 .$$

For width $ch$ the payload is split across a bounded number of tagged calls with the same tags. The reduction from stable sorting to tagged permutations then gives the ordinary-width statement for stable sorting. This completes the proof of Theorem 16.1 (p. 73). ∎
