Definition (bit tensor, network, trivalent, size, volume; table network, bundle, label). pp. 7, 86 paper A bit tensor of arity $u$ over a field is a function $\{0,1\}^u\to$ field. In a tensor network each vertex carries such a table. A wire pairs two argument positions; each unpaired position is a named exterior bit. For a fixed setting of the exterior bits, multiply the selected entries of all vertices and sum over all settings of the wires. The resulting function of the exterior bits is the tensor represented by the network. A network is trivalent if every vertex has arity at most three. For a trivalent network, size counts the vertices and the exterior bits, and volume counts the vertices and all wires including the exterior ones (p. 7).

For unrestricted arity the paper uses the following terms (p. 86). A table network over a field $K$ has bit wires and a table $\{0,1\}^s\to K$ at each station of arity $s$. Its size is the number of stations plus the sum of their arities, including external legs. Parallel bits between two stations form an ordered bundle; the width of a bundle is its number of bits. A label names the table of a station in a dictionary fixed for the network: a rule that assigns a table to each label.

Background — why the three size measures agree up to a constant for trivalent networks. filled here Let a trivalent network have $N$ vertices, $E$ internal wires and $e$ exterior bits. Each internal wire uses two argument positions and each exterior bit one, so $2E+e\le 3N$.
  • Size is $N+e$. Volume is $N+E+e\le N+\tfrac12(3N-e)+e\le\tfrac52(N+e)$. Hence size $\le$ volume $\le\tfrac52\,$size.
  • The table-network size is $N+(2E+e)$. It is at least $N+e$ and at most $4N$. Hence trivalent size $\le$ table-network size $\le 4\,$(trivalent size).
A bound on any one of the three quantities therefore gives a bound on the others with an absolute constant. Theorem 2.2 is stated for volume and Theorem 2.1 for size; §27 starts the macros from the table-network size $s_0\le 4m$.

Why the total arity must fall

Lemma 19.1 turns a network into a sum of networks, each with a few large stations (called frozen) and many small stations (the core). The paper observes (p. 86) that this is not sufficient: the cost of the final evaluation also depends on the total arity. If $u_v$ is the arity of station $v$, the batched evaluation of Lemma 16.2 costs, per copy of the tensor, at most a constant times the number of stations plus

$$\sum_{v:\,u_v\gt 0}u_v^{\,p}\ \le\ \Bigl(\max_vu_v\Bigr)^{p-1}\sum_vu_v .$$

Here $1\lt p\lt 2$ is the exponent of Theorem 16.1(1). The inequality is $u_v^p=u_v^{p-1}u_v\le(\max u)^{p-1}u_v$, summed over $v$. The right side is a product of two factors: a power less than one of the largest arity, and the total arity. The paper's plan is to let the largest arity grow by a factor $h^{(1+\delta)a}$ if the total arity falls by a factor $h^{(1-\delta)a}$. The product then changes by $h^{(1+\delta)a(p-1)-(1-\delta)a}$, and this exponent is negative when $\delta$ is small, because $p-1\lt 1$.

The quantity that controls the trade is the sum of squared arities. Lemma 19.1 takes a bound on this sum as its hypothesis and returns a bound of the same order. The construction keeps the sum of squares within a budget in every round, so that Lemma 19.1 can be applied again.

Definition (summand, block, host, occurrence, grouping step, round, beat). p. 87 paper
  • A summand is one term of the expansion. It is a product of factors called blocks. Different blocks have disjoint lists of external bits.
  • In one summand, its $N_b$ blocks use the same simple graph of possible adjacencies, the host. The vertices of the host are positions; $H$ is their number. The host may depend on the summand.
  • An occurrence is a pair (host position, block). It can be empty. Every actual internal wire joins stations in the same block. A host edge can carry bundles of different widths in different blocks. The local port order at each end of a wire records which bits correspond.
  • A grouping step relabels the blocks and groups them $r$ at a time, with $r$ a power of two. In each group the old blocks are multiplied and transformed together, and the group becomes one new block.
  • A round has three phases: it concentrates the squared arities (Lemma 19.1), then applies a fixed number of wire reductions to the core, then restores bounded degree of the host. A wire reduction is called a beat; a beat consists of several contractions (subrounds) followed by a reset of the core degree (§24).
The number of rounds grows with $h$. Once the parameter $D$ is fixed, all repetition counts within one round are constants.

23.1 The common graph and its initial loads pp. 87–88

Grouping and the host. At a grouping step the new positions may be indexed by an old position, a dyadic subinterval of the $r$ block indices of a group, and a bounded number of other choices. A dyadic interval of $\{0,\dots,r-1\}$ is a set of indices that share a binary prefix; there are $2r-1$ of them. The paper allows padding of the host subject to

$$H'\le c\,r\,H,\qquad N_b'=N_b/r,\tag{23.1}$$

where $c$ is fixed throughout all repetitions of that step and may depend on the design of the round. Consequently the number $HN_b$ of allocated occurrences grows by at most the factor $c$ at each grouping step. This is used in §25.2, display (25.4).

The invariants of a round. At the beginning of each round the paper requires: every live occurrence holds one station (the paper does not define live; the tutorial reads it as: not empty); the host has degree bounded by an absolute constant; external bits are fixed at specified ports. Let $T$ be a dyadic width (a power of two) and $L$ a total arity budget. The charged arity of a live station of actual arity $s_v$ is $z_v=\max(s_v,\theta T)$, where $\theta\gt 0$ is a small fixed dyadic constant chosen in §25.1. The invariants are

$$\sum_vz_v\le L,\qquad\sum_vz_v^2\le LT,\qquad\max_vz_v\le DT/2.\tag{23.2}$$

All three sums and the maximum run over all stations of all blocks of the summand. The paper gives the purpose of each bound (p. 88):

  • the first pays for the surviving bits;
  • the second is the input to the next local concentration (it is the hypothesis $\sum t_i^2\le WT$ of Lemma 19.1);
  • the third lets several stations be packed into one cell of capacity $DT$ without overflow;
  • the floor $\theta T$ bounds the number of stations, and so of table descriptions, that can enter a pack of a given charge.

Every station of arity zero is a scalar. It is removed from the network and its value is kept as a factor of the summand.

First step: bounded degree by binary trees. A station of arity $s$ may be joined to $s$ different stations, so the input graph can have degree $h^b$. The paper replaces each station of positive arity $s$ as follows. The $s$ ports of the station leave it as one ordered bundle to the root of a balanced binary tree. At each split an identity tensor divides one ordered bundle into two bundles whose lengths differ by at most one. At each leaf there is an identity tensor on one bit. The original wires and external bits are attached at the leaves. An input wire that was a loop at one station becomes a connection between two leaves of the same tree.

Background — a balanced binary tree of equality tensors. filled here The identity (or equality) tensor that splits a bundle of $t$ bits is the $0/1$ table on $t+\lceil t/2\rceil+\lfloor t/2\rfloor=2t$ bits which is $1$ exactly when the first $t$ bits equal the concatenation of the other two groups. Three properties are used.
  1. The tensor is unchanged. In the sum over wire values, each identity tensor is zero unless its two sides agree. For given values at the leaves there is exactly one assignment of the tree wires with nonzero weight, and its weight is the original table entry. The network with trees represents the same tensor as before.
  2. Degree and arity ratio. The original station now has one neighbour (the root). A split vertex has three neighbours, a leaf two. A vertex that splits a $t$-bit bundle has arity $2t$ and its children have arity about $t$; the paper records that the arities at the two ends of a tree edge have bounded ratio. A leaf-to-leaf connection has width one at both ends.
  3. Cost. A tree on $s$ ports has $\lceil\log_2s\rceil+1$ levels, and each level has total arity at most $2s$. With $s\le h^b\le h^{1/2}$ there are $O(\log(2+h))$ levels. Summing over stations, the new network has total arity $O(m\log(2+h))$. The new tables are identities described by their lengths, so their labels are short.

Second step: stacking $T_0$ copies. Choose a dyadic $T_0\in[4h^b,8h^b]$ and take $T_0$ copies of the tree network constructed in the first step. At a station of arity $s$ (every arity is now at most $2h^b\le T_0/2$) let $v$ be the largest power of two with $v\le T_0/s$. Divide the $T_0$ copies into consecutive intervals of length $v$. Each interval becomes one position of the host, and the station at that position is the tensor product of the $v$ corresponding copies of the table.

24.2 Subrounds pp. 91–92

In a subround, the occurrences at each position are grouped into packs (a pack is the tensor product of its members, kept as one station). Two outgoing units of width from two different packs of the same colony play the roles of the demanded wires $AB$ and $CD$ of §24.1. The subround must produce many such pairs and must bound the number of packs that one contraction merges.

Balanced partitions (p. 91). Within each group of $r$ block indices, use the dyadic intervals of the indices in their common order. Give every host edge $e$ a propagation distance

$$s_e=64\lceil\log_2(2x_e)\rceil,\qquad x_e=\begin{cases}\Delta&\text{on core edges},\\ \nabla&\text{on tethers}.\end{cases}$$

Let $2\le A\le r$ be dyadic. At each position, mark every interval whose load (the sum of the loads of its occurrences at that position) is greater than $AB$. A marked interval must be split. Marks propagate: a mark passes to the parent interval at the same position, and, for each incident host edge $e$, to the ancestor $s_e$ levels higher at the other end of $e$, whenever that ancestor exists. The maximal unmarked intervals are the leaves of the resulting binary partitions; the paper calls them colonies.

The tagged permutation tensor of §16, $\operatorname{Tag}(y,w_0)$ with $Y=2^y$, has inputs $(j_i,x_i)$ and outputs $x'_j$ (the paper writes $y_j$), where $j_i$ is the $y$-bit destination (tag) of the $w_0$-bit record $x_i$; it is the indicator that the $j_i$ are distinct and $x'_{j_i}=x_i$ for all $i$. If the tags are pinned to a permutation $\pi$, the tensor becomes the product of the equality wires $x'_{\pi(i)}=x_i$. A trivalent network for $\operatorname{Tag}(y,w_0)$ with pinned tags is thus a fixed graph that routes the rows by $\pi$; its vertex tables depend on $\pi$, its graph does not.

The classical example is the Beneš network: for $Y=2^y$ rows it has $2y-1$ columns of $Y/2$ two-by-two switches, and every permutation is obtained by some setting of the switches. Each row bit passes $2y-1$ switches, so the size is of order $Y\,y\,w_0$, that is $c_0=\Theta(y)$. Theorem 16.1(2) gives, under $\omega_k\lt 9/4$ and for wide rows $w_0\ge y^{a_\zeta}$, size $O_\zeta(Yw_0y^{\zeta})$, that is $c_0=O_\zeta(y^\zeta)$ for every $\zeta\gt 0$. §24.4 shows where the smaller $c_0$ is used.

26.1 From a transcript to a tensor pp. 101–103

Definition (processors, records, messages, transcript tensor, charge). p. 101 paper Fix $P\le n^{O(1)}$ numbered processors. Each processor has binary advice of at most $h^c$ bits and a fixed ordered list of exterior ports. Its local record includes guesses and at most $h^c$ incoming and outgoing messages. A message consists of an $O(h)$-bit key identifying source, destination and port, together with a bit string. Communication is valid if every used key occurs exactly once at each end with the same string.

One fixed finite bit-RAM program checks the local record against the advice and the exterior bits. The program may emit strings of length at most $U$ as arguments to a common dictionary $W$ with values in $K$. Its decisions depend only on the strings, not on the values of $W$. A transcript is the collection of all local records. It is accepted if every local check passes and communication is valid. For fixed exterior bits, the transcript tensor is the sum, over accepted transcripts, of the product of all values returned by $W$. The record format, including length fields and padding, is fixed; all size-dependent program data enter through the advice.

The charge at a processor counts its deterministic running time, advice, exterior bits, guesses, messages with headers, and emitted strings, with at least one unit per record or item. The charge is determined by the record. A local address or time uses $(\log h)^{O(1)}$ bits; bit arithmetic on words of that length adds at most a factor $(\log h)^{O(1)}$ to the bounds.

Lemma 26.1 (Realizing a transcript). pp. 101–102 paper Suppose the charge of each local record is at most $V_{\max}=2^{(\log h)^{O(1)}}$. Let $V\le n^{O(1)}$ bound the total charge of every accepted transcript and the volume of the fixed advice and exterior interface, including one unit per processor. If $U\le h$ and the wide-record bound of Theorem 16.1 holds, then for every fixed $\tau\gt 0$ the transcript tensor has a fixed network of size at most $$h^{\tau}\,V+P\,h^{C_0}.$$ The gates are trivalent or are dictionary tables of arity $O(1+U+\log h)$. Their labels use $(\log h)^{O(1)}$ bits. The constant $C_0$ depends only on $\tau,c$, the polynomial bounds in $n$ and the wide-record estimate. The exponent in $V_{\max}$ occurs only in factors $(\log h)^{O(1)}$ before absorption.

The bound has two terms with different roles. The term $h^\tau V$ is nearly linear in the total charge: work that is proportional to the data costs only the factor $h^\tau$. The term $Ph^{C_0}$ is a fixed overhead per processor with a possibly large exponent. Subsection 26.2 makes the second term small by having few processors relative to the data.

Background — a checked computation with guesses as a tensor network. filled here A Boolean circuit becomes a tensor network when each gate is replaced by its $0/1$ truth table (value $1$ exactly when the output wire equals the gate function of the input wires) and each fan-out by an equality tensor. For fixed inputs there is exactly one assignment of the internal wires with weight $1$; all others have weight $0$. If the output is pinned to "accept", the contraction is $1$ on accepted inputs and $0$ otherwise.

Now leave some input wires unpaired with the exterior and sum over them: these are guesses. The contraction counts the guesses that the circuit accepts. If, in addition, one-bit or table gates with values in $K$ are attached to some wires, the contraction is the sum over accepted guesses of the product of those values. This is the form of the transcript tensor: guesses are the records, the $0/1$ gates are the checks, and the $K$-valued gates are the dictionary calls. Exactness requires that each accepted transcript corresponds to exactly one assignment of all internal wires. Every step of the proof below includes a uniqueness clause for this reason. The $0/1$ tables are tensors over the prime field, hence over $k$ and over $K$.

Background — sorting by a comparison network. filled here A comparison network on $N$ records is a fixed sequence of compare-exchange operations on fixed pairs of positions. The positions do not depend on the data, so the network is a fixed circuit. Batcher's network sorts $N$ records with $O(N\log^2N)$ comparators (the paper cites it in Proposition 2.3 and uses the count $O(t\log^2(2t))$ here). A comparator on words of $\omega$ bits is $O(\omega)$ Boolean gates. To use an unstable sorting network where the order of equal keys matters, one appends a distinguishing field (here the execution time) to the key. The cost per record is the factor $\log^2N$ times the word length. This is affordable when $\log N$ is $(\log h)^{O(1)}$, as inside one slot below, and not affordable when $\log N$ is of order $h$, as for the global matching of messages.

Step 1: allocate space by record length (p. 102). The network is fixed before the transcript is known, so it cannot give each processor exactly the space its record needs. The paper reserves slots in dyadic size classes. For each dyadic capacity $t$, from $1$ to the least power of two at least $\min(V,V_{\max})$, it reserves $N_t=\min(P,\lfloor 2V/t\rfloor)$ slots. A processor with charge $s$ uses the least $t\ge s$. The network guesses, for each slot, a processor identifier and a record; it checks the identifier range and the charge, requires the occupied slots to form an increasing prefix in identifier order (within each capacity class, in this tutorial's reading), and pins all unused entries and padding. The two capacity bounds are

$$\sum_tt\,N_t=O\bigl(V(1+\log V_{\max})\bigr),\qquad\sum_tN_t=O\bigl(P(1+\log V_{\max})\bigr).\tag{26.1}$$

Step 2: check the computation and its dictionary arguments (p. 102). In a slot of capacity $t$ the network guesses the deterministic execution of the checking program for at most $t$ steps and verifies it by the trace construction of [42, Section 16.2]: time-ordered checks of the processor state, and a memory check by sorting the accesses. A comparison network on $O(t)$ accesses uses $O(t\log^2(2t))$ comparisons of $(\log h)^{O(1)}$-bit words. Long inputs, advice, messages and emitted strings occupy addressed arrays and are processed in pieces. The local cost is $t(\log h)^{O(1)}$ plus $h^{O(1)}$ per slot for long identifiers and framing.

The dictionary calls are installed as follows. Within the slot, the emitted strings are grouped by least dyadic padded length $u$ (an empty string counts as length one). The slot reserves $\lfloor 2t/u\rfloor$ seats in that class and fills them in order of issuance. The checker writes each emitted bit at the address given by its length class, ordinal and bit offset; the same memory-trace test compares these arrays with the inputs of the installed seats, including active flags, actual lengths and zero padding. Each address has $O(1+\log t+\log h)$ bits. A seat returns the value of $W$ on its string when active and returns $1$ when inactive. Its table is named by the padded length. The arity of a seat is $O(1+U+\log h)$, and the total table size per slot is $O(t\log(2+U)\log(2+h))$.

Step 3: match the messages (pp. 102–103). Fix $w=\lceil h^{C_1}\rceil$ with $C_1\gt 1$ chosen below. Each message is split into $w$-bit chunks; the last chunk is padded, and an empty message gets one chunk. A chunk record contains an active flag, the key, the direction (send or receive), the total length, the chunk number and the payload. For each processor a fixed sender with a key in a reserved namespace supplies that processor's advice and exterior bits with their component lengths; an occupied slot must receive the whole supply for its claimed identifier, even when the supply is empty.

Local checks compare each outgoing source and incoming destination with the slot identifier and verify the splitting, piece order, lengths and padding. Then all chunk records of all slots are sorted by active flag, key, chunk number and direction. In the active part every key and chunk number must occur as one send and one receive with equal length and payload; a scan of adjacent records checks this. A duplicate message on one side produces a collision at chunk zero. In particular each private supply is matched to exactly one slot, so every processor occurs exactly once among the slots.

The sort is the stable-sort tensor of Theorem 16.1(2). For a given input the stable-sort relation has exactly one output, so the sorted list can be an internal (summed) set of wires without creating multiplicity. Its $0/1$ tensor over $k$ is the same relation over $K$.

Step 4: count (p. 103). A slot of capacity $t$ reserves $O(t/w+h^c+1)$ chunk records. The $O(h)$-bit header is copied once per chunk, so constructing the records costs $(t+h^{O(1)})(\log h)^{O(1)}$. A fixed sender is charged for its actual supply and one partial chunk. By (26.1) the reserved bit volume, including padding at message boundaries, is

$$V(\log h)^{O(1)}+P\,h^{O(1)}.$$

The list is padded to a power of two. Its length is polynomial in $n$, so the logarithm of its length is $O(h)$. Theorem 16.1(2) with tolerance $\tau/3$ gives an exponent $a_{\tau/3}$; a fixed $C_1$ large enough makes $w$ at least the required power of the logarithm of the list length, and then sorting costs at most $O(h^{\tau/3})$ times the volume. The scan after the sort has linear size. By (26.1) the local computations and the dictionary tables have the same volume bound. The powers of $\log h$ are absorbed into $h^\tau$ and the per-processor costs into $Ph^{C_0}$.

Step 5: exactness. The checking and sorting gates are trivalent over $k$. Each dictionary gate is named by its length class, in $(\log h)^{O(1)}$ bits. For each accepted transcript, the slot capacities, identifiers, order, traces, calls and chunks are uniquely determined; conversely the local and communication checks recover an accepted transcript from any assignment of nonzero weight. Contracting the network therefore gives the weighted sum over accepted transcripts, exactly, over $K$.

26.2 The transcript of the rounds pp. 103–106

The paper now describes the construction of §23–§25 as a transcript. Initially there are $S$ blocks of $T_0$ copies. At every stage of the $j$ rounds there are $N_b\ge R$ blocks on a host with $H$ allocated positions. Each position gets one processor at each stage, including the terminal stage, and each step gets $O(H)$ additional processors for balanced aggregation trees. An occurrence is one position in one block.

The cost analysis separates three kinds of data, with three different unit costs. The paper states the principle in the introduction of the section (p. 101): "All blocks at one host position use the same address. The bits carried on a wire vary independently across blocks."

One address for all blocks (p. 103). A position holds the ordered list of its host neighbours and an array of occurrence records in block order. Its advice specifies the parent and child naming conventions, integer parameters, the presentation of the field $K$, and the initial convention for ordering exterior ports; at an initial position it also gives the input table label. Exterior bits enter through fixed ports in stack order.

In one step every host degree is bounded in terms of the round design, so a ball of radius $O_D(1+\log r)$ contains $r^{O_D(1)}$ positions. Positions exchange neighbour lists to that radius, remove repetitions by identifier, and order the names. After this, each possible child and contact is located by an index in a local list; the cost is $h^{O(1)}$ common operations per position. A message to a contact can then carry the entire array of occurrence records, for all blocks, under one $O(h)$-bit header, and inside the array only local indices are needed. There are $h^{O(1)}$ phases and declarations per position. By (25.6) the handling of the arrays costs

$$N_b\,r^{O_D(1)}(\log h)^{O(1)}h^{O_D(a)}=N_b\,h^{O_D(a)+o(1)}$$

per position. The paper states that the coefficients in $O_D(a)$ are independent of $a,b,\beta$.

Decisions shared by the host (pp. 103–104). Several choices in a round are common to all blocks and are made by minimizing a score that is a sum of local contributions: the multiplier of the colony count (24.1), the affine colouring, the layout of a subround, the mixing matrix of a reset. Each position computes its local contributions to the scores of all candidates. The integer arrays are added along a balanced tree, and the first minimizing candidate is broadcast. At height $i$ of the tree a sum of entries that initially had $z$ bits has at most $z+i$ bits, while the number of tree vertices falls by a factor of order $2^i$; since $\sum_{i\ge 0}2^{-i}(z+i)^{O(1)}=O((1+z)^{O(1)})$, the aggregation costs no more than the leaves, up to a constant. At the root the paper allows $2^{(\log h)^{O(1)}}$ additional operations to choose the minimizer. A greedy colouring of a host (needed by conclusion 4 of Lemma 19.1) is checked locally: in fixed identifier order, each colour must be the least one absent from the lower-numbered neighbours; induction on identifiers shows that exactly one assignment passes the check.

Checking the changes of host (pp. 104–105). The paper lists how each operation of a round obtains its contacts from the neighbour lists and its common parameters from additive scores.

  1. Colony packing (§24.2). Try the multipliers of the $r$ slots, propagate the overloaded marks to ancestors and neighbours, and sum the candidate colony counts. A propagation moves upward by at least one dyadic level, so the radius is $O_D(1+\log r)$. Relative interval endpoints, loads and dyadically scaled charges have short encodings. Summing local scores also chooses the affine colouring for the whole-item pairing.
  2. Local concentration (§25.1). The colony interval and its old contacts give the possible neighbours of each sphere (the sphere forms of §19, one family per cell, are the objects on which the proof of Lemma 19.1 operates). In the checked colour order, the movement and interpolation choices of Lemma 19.1 specify the new tables, withdrawn scalars and ordered bundles. The description bounds of Lemma 19.1 (its conclusion 4) pay for the choices and labels. The port orders identify the exposed sublists.
  3. Wire-saving subround (§24.2). The source colony and bin determine the seats of its matched units. One summand choice is recorded for each actual gadget. The surviving replacement widths under each of the two contractions are summed, and the same minimizer is taken for all blocks. Bounded neighbourhoods of the colonies determine the new contacts, port orders and table labels.
  4. Reset (§24.3). Each position computes its mean load over the blocks. The smoothed mean has floor $FB/r$, and a propagated contribution decays by at least a factor of two per edge; since the starting load is at most $B$, a neighbourhood of radius $O(1+\log r)$ determines the bases and their capacities. The common mixing matrix is chosen as described in the next box. Positions permute their block arrays accordingly. Bounded length ratios of intersecting leaves and the bounded number of units per leaf allow the terminal assignments, rotor settings and new labels to be determined from neighbouring records.
  5. Tether trees (§25.1). The final splitting trees have bounded size, depending on $D$, per possible station and contact. Adjacent positions determine the active sublists. An edge between new children is supported by a bounded path between their parents, so every neighbourhood that is collected is local to its current step.

Messages are declared in a fixed order of phases, ports and blocks. Each successive array and host is enforced from the preceding data and the local summand choices. Each term of a local identity, including a term of weight zero, has one prescribed encoding, so the intermediate arrays introduce no further multiplicities.

$$\begin{aligned}P&\le m\,S\,h^{O_D(a)+o(1)}/R,\\ V&\le P\,h^{C_2}+m\,S\,h^{O_D(a)+o(1)}+(L_j+ed)\,h^{o(1)}+2^{(\log h)^{O(1)}}.\end{aligned}\tag{26.2}$$

Every local record has charge at most $2^{(\log h)^{O(1)}}$, because $N_b\le S$, $\log S=O((\log h)^2)$, and one occurrence has polynomially many bits in $h$; so the hypothesis on $V_{\max}$ in Lemma 26.1 holds. Throughout, $k$ is a finite field with $\omega_k\lt 9/4$ (the hypothesis that the paper refutes), $n$ is a large integer, $h=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$, and logarithms are binary. A table network over a field $K$ has stations joined by wires that each carry one bit; a station of arity $s$ carries a table $\{0,1\}^s\to K$; the network represents the tensor obtained by multiplying the selected table entries and summing over all values of the internal wires. Its size is the number of stations plus the sum of their arities. A network is trivalent when every arity is at most three. For a trivalent network the size in Theorem 2.1 counts the vertices and the exterior bits; this count and the table-network size differ by a factor of at most $4$. These definitions and the factor are given in full in §23.

The section has three parts: the three statements it combines, Lemma 27.1 with its proof, and the proof of Theorem 2.1. The proof of Theorem 2.1 is exponent bookkeeping. It is written out below with every parameter, the constraint on it, and the order of choice.

Background — table lookup by sorting, in the form used here. filled here A trivalent network cannot hold a table of arity $t\gt 3$ at one vertex. The proof of Lemma 16.2 (p. 59) evaluates $2^t$ copies of one table $f:\{0,1\}^t\to K$ together. The steps are as follows.
  1. The $2^t$ query addresses $x_1,\dots,x_{2^t}$ are the exposed legs. The required value is $\prod_i f(x_i)=\prod_z f(z)^{c_z}$, where $c_z$ is the number of queries with address $z$.
  2. For each address $z$ there is one dictionary row. The network guesses $c_z$ in $t+1$ bits. On count bit $j$ ($0\le j\le t$) it places a one-bit tensor with the two entries $(1,\ f(z)^{2^j})$. The product of these tensors over the bits of $c_z$ is $f(z)^{c_z}$.
  3. Each query also gets a row, with zeros in the count field. The $2^{t+1}$ rows (queries and dictionary rows) are sorted by address and then by row type, so that the dictionary row of an address follows all queries to that address. A counter starts at zero and increments on every query row; on a dictionary row it must agree with the guessed count, and it then resets to zero. This forces every guessed count to be the true multiplicity. For fixed exposed addresses there is exactly one consistent assignment of all internal wires, so the contraction equals $\prod_i f(x_i)$.
  4. The rows have $O(t+1)$ bits. By Theorem 16.1(1), the sort has size $O(2^t(1+t)^p)$; the counter has size $O(2^t(1+t))$.
A network with stations $v$ of arities $t_v\le u$ is copied $2^u$ times; the copies of station $v$ are grouped into $2^{u-t_v}$ batches of $2^{t_v}$. The total size is at most a constant times $$2^u\sum_v(1+t_v)^p\ \le\ 2^u(1+u)^{p-1}\sum_v(1+t_v).$$ The cost per unit of arity is therefore $(1+u)^{p-1}$, where $u$ is the largest arity. This is the only place in Part IV where the inequality $p\lt 2$ of Theorem 16.1(1) is used. The other uses of Theorem 16.1 in Part IV (the rotor in §24.3 and the message sort in §26.1) use part (2), the wide-record bound.

The local tensor identities of Part III need a field $K\supseteq k$ with at least a fixed power of $T$ elements, where $T$ is the arity scale. The networks produced by the macros therefore have tables with values in $K$, although the tensor $V^{\otimes d}$ that they represent has entries in $k$. Theorem 2.1 asks for a network over $k$. Lemma 27.1 (Descent of a trivalent network). p. 106 paper Let $K/k$ have finite degree $D$. If a trivalent $K$-network of size $s$ represents a tensor with entries in $k$, the same tensor has a trivalent $k$-network of size $O(sD^2)$ with the same exterior positions.

In this lemma $D$ denotes the degree $[K:k]$. In §25 the same letter denotes the concentration parameter. The two are unrelated.

Background — multiplication in $K$ as a $D\times D$ matrix over $k$. filled here Choose a $k$-basis $\varepsilon_1=1,\varepsilon_2,\dots,\varepsilon_D$ of $K$. For $\lambda\in K$ define the matrix $M_\lambda\in k^{D\times D}$ by $\lambda\,\varepsilon_j=\sum_i (M_\lambda)_{ij}\,\varepsilon_i$. Three facts follow from the definition.
  1. $M_{\lambda\mu}=M_\lambda M_\mu$, because multiplication by $\lambda\mu$ is the composition of the two multiplications.
  2. The first column of $M_\lambda$ is the coordinate vector of $\lambda\cdot\varepsilon_1=\lambda$. Hence $(M_\lambda)_{11}=\pi(\lambda)$, where $\pi:K\to k$ sends an element to its first coordinate.
  3. The map $\pi$ is $k$-linear, and $\pi(c)=c$ for $c\in k$, because $c=c\,\varepsilon_1$.
The map $\pi$ is not multiplicative. The proof below applies it once, to a complete product, and uses matrix products for everything before that.

Step 1: a chain through all stations. Number the stations $v_1,\dots,v_N$ in an arbitrary order; $N\le s$, because the size $s$ counts the stations. The order need not be related to the wiring. Give station $v_t$ two additional ports: an incoming index $i_{t-1}$ and an outgoing index $i_t$, each in $\{1,\dots,D\}$. Join the outgoing port of $v_t$ to the incoming port of $v_{t+1}$. Fix an assignment $x$ of all original wires and exterior bits. Under $x$, station $v_t$ selects a table entry $\lambda_t\in K$. The enlarged table at $v_t$ returns the entry $(M_{\lambda_t})_{i_t\,i_{t-1}}\in k$. Pin $i_0=1$ and $i_N=1$.

Step 2: the chain computes one coordinate of the product. For the fixed $x$, summing over $i_1,\dots,i_{N-1}$ is a product of matrices: $$\sum_{i_1,\dots,i_{N-1}}\ \prod_{t=1}^{N}(M_{\lambda_t})_{i_t i_{t-1}}=\bigl(M_{\lambda_N}\cdots M_{\lambda_1}\bigr)_{11}=\bigl(M_{\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N}\bigr)_{11}=\pi(\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N).$$ The second equality is fact 1 of the background box, and the third is fact 2.

Step 3: linearity moves $\pi$ outside the sum over wires. The entry of the original tensor at given exterior bits is $\sum_x\prod_t\lambda_t(x)$, where $x$ runs over the internal wires. The new network gives $\sum_x\pi\bigl(\prod_t\lambda_t(x)\bigr)=\pi\bigl(\sum_x\prod_t\lambda_t(x)\bigr)$ by fact 3. The argument of $\pi$ is the original entry, which lies in $k$ by hypothesis, so $\pi$ returns it unchanged. The new network has entries in $k$ and represents the same tensor on the same exterior positions.

Step 4: the enlarged tables are made trivalent. Encode each index in $\lceil\log_2 D\rceil$ bits; codes that name no basis element get weight zero. A trivalent station has at most three original ports, so the enlarged table has at most $r=3+2\lceil\log_2 D\rceil$ bits. The paper states that every table on $r$ bits has a trivalent realization of size $O(2^r)$ (construction in the next box). Since $2^{\lceil\log_2 D\rceil}\lt 2D$, this is $2^r\lt 8\,(2D)^2=32D^2$ per original station, and $O(sD^2)$ in total.

Proof of Theorem 2.1: the steps

Step 1: one field for all macros (p. 107). The scales $T$ that occur in the $J$ macros lie between a constant times $h^\beta$ and a fixed power of $h$. The local identities (Lemma 19.1) require a fixed finite extension of $k$ and a field of cardinality at least a fixed power of $T$. Choose $K\supseteq k$ with $[K:k]$ divisible by the degrees of the required fixed extensions and large enough for the largest such power. Because $k$ is finite, $|K|=|k|^{[K:k]}$, so $[K:k]=O(\log h)$ suffices; and $\log T\ge\beta\log h-O(1)$ at every scale, so $[K:k]=O(\log T)$ as Lemma 19.1 requires.

Step 2: the induction over macros. Let $s_j$ be the size (stations plus total arity) after $j$ macros, $d_j$ the cumulative number of copies, and $e_j=d_je$ the number of exterior bits. The network after $j$ macros represents $V^{\otimes d_j}$.

Start. $d_0=1$. The given network is trivalent of size $m$, so it has at most $m$ vertices of arity at most $3$; hence $s_0\le 4m$. Its arity is at most $3\le h^{\beta}$ for large $n$. The trivalent $k$-tables form a fixed finite dictionary, so labels have $O(1)$ bits.

Step $j\lt J$. Lemma 23.1 needs an input with at most $m\,h^{-2a}$ exterior bits. The current network may have fewer than $h^{2a}$ units of size per exterior bit. The paper therefore applies the lemma with the size allowance $$M_j=\max\bigl\{s_j,\ \lceil e_jh^{2a}\rceil\bigr\}$$ in the role of the lemma's "$m$". A network of size $s_j$ is also a network of size at most $M_j$. The hypotheses of Lemma 23.1 hold with $m:=M_j$, $e:=e_j$, $b:=\beta+(1+\delta)aj$:

  • $e_j\le M_jh^{-2a}$ by the definition of $M_j$;
  • $M_j\ge e_jh^{2a}\ge e\ge n$;
  • $M_j\le n^{1.1}$ (next box);
  • the arity bound is $h^{\beta+(1+\delta)aj}$ with $\beta\le\beta+(1+\delta)aj\le b_*\lt 1/2$, by the first condition of (27.1);
  • labels have $(\log h)^{O(1)}$ bits, by the conclusion of the previous macro.

Step 3: the recurrence per exterior bit. Put $x_j=s_j/e_j$. The represented tensor is $V^{\otimes d}$ with $$d=2^u\,d_J,\qquad \log_2d\le h^{b_*}+O\bigl(J(\log h)^2\bigr)=o(h).$$ The last equality uses $b_*\lt 1$. Hence $d=n^{o(1)}$, uniformly in the input network for fixed $k,\eta$. Each of the $de$ exterior positions remains exterior. The size per exterior bit is now at most $O((1+u)^{\rho})\,x_J=O(h^{\rho b_*})\,x_J$.

How the theorem closes the argument

The final paragraph of the paper's text (p. 107) connects Theorem 2.1 to the lower bound. The tensor $D_w$ is the $0/1$ tensor of list distinctness: its input is a list of $2^w$ words of $2w$ bits, and its value is $1$ exactly when the words are pairwise distinct. It has $n_w=2w\,2^w$ exterior bits. Theorem 2.2 (p. 7) states that there is $\sigma\gt 0$, depending only on $k$, with $\operatorname{vol}_k(D_w^{\otimes t})\gt t\,n_w\,w^{\sigma}$ for all large $w$ and all $1\le t\le 2^w$; here $\operatorname{vol}_k$ is the minimum trivalent volume over $k$, which agrees with trivalent size up to an absolute factor (§23).

Proposition 2.3 (p. 8) chooses $0\lt\eta\lt\sigma$, takes $\gamma$ from Theorem 2.1, and fixes an integer greater than $2/\gamma$; the paper calls this integer $J$ as well, and it is a different number from the $J$ of this section. Write it $J'$. The starting network is a Batcher sorting network followed by adjacent comparisons, of size $O(n_ww^2)$; its size per exterior bit is $x_0=O(w^2)$. With $n=n_w$ and $h=w+O(\log w)$, each application of (2.1) gives

$$x_{j+1}\le h^{-\gamma}x_j+h^{\eta}.$$

After $J'$ applications, $x_{J'}\le h^{-J'\gamma}x_0+h^{\eta}\sum_{r\ge 0}h^{-r\gamma}=O(w^{2-J'\gamma})+O(w^{\eta})=O(w^{\eta})$, because $J'\gamma\gt 2$. The number of lists is $t_{J'}=n^{o(1)}\le 2^w$. The resulting network for $D_w^{\otimes t_{J'}}$ has volume $O(t_{J'}n_ww^{\eta})$, which is less than $t_{J'}n_ww^{\sigma}$ for large $w$. This contradicts Theorem 2.2. The paper concludes that $\omega_k\lt 9/4$ is false for every finite field $k$.

The independence of the $de$ exterior positions in Theorem 2.1 is used here: the exterior positions of $D_w^{\otimes t}$ are the entries of $t$ separately specified lists, and Theorem 2.2 is uniform in $t$. The uniformity is needed because the replication factor $d$ is chosen by the compression and not by the user of the theorem.
