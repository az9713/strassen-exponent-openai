19. Concentrating the arity of a tensor network pp. 73–76

Role in the chain. Part III of the paper (§19–§22, pp. 73–86) proves one statement, Lemma 19.1 (Local concentration). It receives from §13 the fermionic sphere tensors, Proposition 13.1 (Fusion, p. 48) and Lemma 13.2 (Dimension of a level, p. 49); from §14 Corollary 14.4 (A common comparison tensor, p. 55); and from §3 the transport bound (3.1) and two results that the paper imports from [42]. Lemma 19.2, Lemma 20.1 and the identities of §21 are algebra that holds without the hypothesis. Lemma 19.1 is applied in §25.1 (p. 96), once in every round, with $\ell=10$ and $W=C_1L$, inside the proof of Lemma 23.1 (Compression of a tensor power, p. 86); §27 applies that lemma repeatedly to prove Theorem 2.1 (Compression of tensor powers, p. 7). Section 19 itself states Lemma 19.1, fixes the multi-species sphere forms, proves the dimension bound (19.1), and proves Lemma 19.2, which §21.2 uses to carry an insertion from one cell to the next.

The paper states the aim on p. 73: gather the large tables of a tensor network at a few stations, without changing the tensor that the network represents, and keep the sum of squared arities bounded while doing so. The four sections of Part III are one proof. This section gives the statement, the plan, and the algebraic tools.

Definition (station, arity, cell, host, immobile). p. 73 paper A station is a tensor given by an arbitrary table on an ordered list of bits: a function $\{0,1\}^t\to K$, where $K$ is the coefficient field. Its arity $t$ counts its external bits and its ends of internal wires. The stations are grouped into cells. The cells are the vertices of a host graph of maximum degree $g$. Every wire runs inside one cell, or between two adjacent cells, or to the exterior. One host may carry several networks at once; a cell used by two networks counts twice. Lemma 19.1 counts occupied cells; by the previous sentence, a cell is counted once for each network that uses it. A station designated immobile keeps its external bits and its cell throughout the construction. Every external bit must belong to an immobile station.
Background — arity, contraction, and why merging stations costs arity. filled here A table network has stations joined by binary wires. For a fixed setting of the external bits, its value is the sum, over all settings of the internal wire bits, of the product of the table entries. This sum is the contraction of the network. Two stations of arities $t_1,t_2$ joined by $w$ wires can be replaced by one station whose table is the product of the two tables summed over the $w$ shared bits; the new arity is $t_1+t_2-2w$. The contraction is unchanged. A table of arity $t$ has $2^t$ entries, so the cost of using a station grows with its arity. Lemma 16.2 (Batched table evaluation, p. 59) charges a station of arity $t_v$ about $(1+t_v)^p$ per copy with an exponent $1\lt p\lt 2$ (see §16). Merging stations in this way adds their arities, up to the shared wires. The construction of Part III merges stations in a different way. Each station is attached to the rest of the network through one leg of a sphere form, and when two stations with legs of widths $w_1,w_2$ are merged, the leg of the merged station has width about $\sqrt{w_1^2+w_2^2}$ and not $w_1+w_2$. The price is that the one network is replaced by a sum of many networks.
Gap fill — the budget arithmetic of the paper's first paragraph. filled here The paper's example (p. 73) has $W/T$ stations of arity $T$. Three computations give its three numbers.
  1. The sum of squared arities is $(W/T)\cdot T^2=WT$.
  2. If $D$ stations at a time are merged with arities added, there are $W/(DT)$ stations of arity $DT$, and the sum of squares is $\frac{W}{DT}(DT)^2=WDT$. The budget is multiplied by $D$.
  3. If instead there are $N=O(W/(DT))$ stations whose squared arities still sum to $O(WT)$, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives $$\begin{aligned}\sum_{i=1}^N t_i&\le\Bigl(N\sum_i t_i^2\Bigr)^{1/2}\\&=\Bigl(O\bigl(\tfrac{W}{DT}\bigr)\cdot O(WT)\Bigr)^{1/2}=O\bigl(W/\sqrt D\bigr).\end{aligned}$$
The total arity at the large stations falls from $W$ to $O(W/\sqrt D)$. Lemma 19.1 asserts that case 3 can be reached.
Lemma 19.1 (Local concentration). p. 73 paper Fix a finite field $k$ with $\omega_k\lt 9/4$, a degree bound $g$, and an integer $\ell\ge 1$. For each sufficiently large fixed integer $D$, the following holds for all sufficiently large integers $T$. Use an extension $K/k$ containing a fixed finite extension, with $|K|$ at least a sufficiently large polynomial in $T$ and $[K:k]=O_D(\log T)$. The input tables may already be over $K$.

Suppose there are at most $W/(DT)$ occupied cells and at most $W/(DT)$ immobiles in total. In any one network each cell has at most $100D$ stations of total arity at most $100DT$, and over all stations of all networks $\sum_i t_i^2\le WT$. Then the tensor of each network is a sum over $K$ of networks with the same exposed bits. Every choice of one term per network, taken together, satisfies the following bounds.

  1. There are $O(W/(DT))$ frozen stations, including the holders of the original external bits in their original cells. Their maximum arity is $O(DT)$, their squared arities sum to $O(WT)$, and their arities sum to $O(W/\sqrt D)$.
  2. All other stations, called the core, have arity at most $D^{-\ell}T$ and total arity $O\bigl(W(1+\log D)^2\bigr)$.
  3. A common host replaces each original cell by $D^{O(1)}$ positions, with possible contacts only within and between neighboring cells. The induced host on the core positions has absolute bounded degree. No two frozen stations touch by a wire. For some $c=c(g,\ell,k)$ all active internal bundles can have width at least $\lceil T/D^{c}\rceil$, and the bounds in the first two conclusions continue to hold when every station is charged at least that arity.
  4. The outer summation uses $O_D(\log(T+1))$ control bits per occupied cell. For its computational bound, represent each input table by a label and its ports by ordered bundles, one per station pair, specified by lengths and endpoint slots. Bits agree in order along a bundle. Any local reordering is part of the supplied table label. Given a fixed coloring with $O_D(1)$ colors separating cells within a bounded distance depending on $D,g$, the validity of the indices, output layout, and tracking of exposed sublists can be determined in $O_D(1)$ rounds of communication on the old host. With input labels of at most $A$ bits, the work and communication per occupied cell are $O_D\bigl(A+(1+\log T)^{O(1)}\bigr)$ bit operations and bits. Each output table has a label of length $O_D(A+\log(T+1))$ in a dictionary fixed by the input dictionary and the parameters.
The implicit constants in the first three conclusions can depend on $k,g,\ell$, independently of $D,T,W$. A fixed change of the numerical input bound $100$ changes only these constants. (The statement begins on p. 73; the four conclusions are on p. 74.)

Notation in the statement. $\omega_k$ is the exponent of matrix multiplication over $k$: the infimum of the numbers $\tau$ such that two $n\times n$ matrices over $k$ can be multiplied with $O(n^\tau)$ arithmetic operations. A subscript on an $O$ names the parameters on which the implied constant may depend: $O_D(\cdot)$ may depend on $D$ (and on $k,g,\ell$), $O_g(\cdot)$ on $g$, and $O_{g,\ell}(\cdot)$ on $g$, $\ell$ and the characteristic. The paper uses "external bits" and "exposed bits" for the same thing, the bits of a network that are not summed. A label is a bit string that names the table of a station in a dictionary, a list of tables fixed for the network (§23, p. 86); the network description carries labels and not table entries.

How to read the statement. The order of the quantifiers is: first $k,g,\ell$; then $D$, large in terms of $k,g,\ell$; then $T$, large in terms of $D$. The number $W$ is not required to be large; it enters only through the four hypotheses. The constants of conclusions 1–3 do not depend on $D,T,W$. The thresholds are not effective as given: the threshold for $T$ depends on an internal block size $b$, which must exceed a bound $b_0$ that comes from a limit without a stated rate (Corollary 14.4), and the $O_D$ constants of conclusion 4 depend on $b$ and on constants $c_\tau$ imported from [42] (see §20.2). In the terms of Table 1 of §23 (p. 87), $T$ is the working arity scale and $D$ is the concentration parameter. The hypotheses say that the input looks like the example above after a coarse grouping: at most $W/(DT)$ cells, each holding at most $100D$ stations of total arity at most $100DT$, with squared arities summing to at most $WT$.

The conclusion does not bound the total arity below its input value. The hypotheses give at most $100W/T$ stations, so by Cauchy–Schwarz the input total is at most $\sqrt{(100W/T)\cdot WT}=10W$. The bound for the core alone, $O(W(1+\log D)^2)$, is larger than this. What the lemma delivers is a separation into two kinds of station. The frozen stations are few, $O(W/(DT))$, and carry total arity $O(W/\sqrt D)$. The core stations are small, each of arity at most $D^{-\ell}T$. Part IV then shortens the wires among the core stations (§24) before the next application of the lemma.

Two notations in the statement need a remark. A bundle is the ordered list of all parallel wires between one pair of stations. "A sum over $K$ of networks" means that the tensor $V$ of an input network is written as $V=\sum_{\beta}V_\beta$, where $\beta$ runs over the values of a string of control bits, and each $V_\beta$ is the tensor of a table network over $K$ on the same exposed bits (coefficients in $K$ are stored in the tables). The bounds 1–3 hold for every $\beta$. Conclusion 4 says that $\beta$ is short and that the shape of the network $V_\beta$ can be computed from $\beta$ by local work.

AHA. The construction represents a station of arity $t$ by a vector space $V_e$ of dimension about $2^{t}$, graded by an energy $e$ with $t\approx\text{const}\cdot\sqrt e$ (display (19.1) below). When two such objects collide, the Laurent coefficients of negative order pass through a space $V_{e'}$ with $e'\lt e_1+e_2$ (Proposition 13.1). Energy is therefore subadditive under merging, and energy is proportional to squared arity. This is the reason why the sum of squared arities, and not the sum of arities, is the quantity that the construction can hold fixed.

Plan of the proof (pp. 74–86). The paper gives the plan in two paragraphs on p. 74. In each occupied cell it uses two sphere forms. The first form joins neighboring cells along pairs of legs called seams, and represents each station by an insertion at a marked point. The second form stays in its cell and serves to realize the actual wires of the input network. The table shows where each step is carried out.

19.1 Sphere forms and a transposition identity

This subsection (pp. 74–76) extends the construction of §13 from one species of fermion to $r$ species, records the dimension bound, restates fusion for two sites, defines a seam, and proves Lemma 19.2.

Background — exterior algebra, Wick's rule and Pfaffians. filled here Let $E$ be a vector space with an ordered basis $(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\ldots)$. The exterior algebra $\Lambda(E)$ has the basis of wedges $\varepsilon_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\varepsilon_{i_d}$ with $i_1\lt\cdots\lt i_d$; the number $d$ is the length of the wedge. The product is associative and $\varepsilon_i\wedge\varepsilon_j=-\varepsilon_j\wedge\varepsilon_i$, $\varepsilon_i\wedge\varepsilon_i=0$. Two facts are used below. (i) $\Lambda(E_1\oplus E_2)\cong\Lambda(E_1)\otimes\Lambda(E_2)$: a wedge is a wedge of generators of $E_1$ followed by a wedge of generators of $E_2$. (ii) Covectors $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d\in E^*$ define a linear functional on length-$d$ wedges by $v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_d\mapsto\det[\lambda_s(v_t)]$; if the $\lambda$ range over a spanning set of $E^*$, these functionals span the dual of the space of length-$d$ wedges.

Let $g_1,\ldots,g_{2n}$ be a list of symbols and let $K_{st}$ be numbers with $K_{ts}=-K_{st}$. The Pfaffian is $$\operatorname{Pf}(K)=\sum_{\text{perfect matchings}}\operatorname{sgn}\cdot\prod_{r=1}^{n}K_{s_rt_r},$$ where a matching is a partition of the list into pairs $(s_r,t_r)$ with $s_r\lt t_r$, and $\operatorname{sgn}$ is the sign of the permutation that sends $(1,\ldots,2n)$ to $(s_1,t_1,\ldots,s_n,t_n)$. For four symbols, $\operatorname{Pf}(K)=K_{12}K_{34}-K_{13}K_{24}+K_{14}K_{23}$; this is the four-point example of §13.1 (p. 47). Wick's rule is the name for a form defined by such a signed sum over pairings, with $K_{st}$ called the contraction of $g_s$ with $g_t$. Three properties are used repeatedly. (P1) The Pfaffian is linear in each symbol: if $g_s$ is replaced by a linear combination of symbols, row and column $s$ of $K$ change linearly. (P2) Reordering the list by a permutation multiplies the Pfaffian by the sign of the permutation. (P3) If the list is split into groups, the matchings can be sorted by the set of pairs that lie inside the groups; this is the step behind fusion.

Definition (species, mode, energy, the spaces $V^{(r)}_e$). p. 74 paper Take the exterior algebra on generators $\psi_{s,m}$ with species $1\le s\le r$ and mode $m\ge 0$. The generator $\psi_{s,m}$ has energy $m+\tfrac12$; the energy of a wedge is the sum of the energies of its generators. $V^{(r)}_e$ is the span of the wedges of energy $e\in\tfrac12\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}$. Its basis is the wedge basis, with generators in lexicographic order on $(s,m)$. Every basis vector has length congruent to $2e$ modulo $2$, because a wedge of length $d$ has energy $d/2$ plus an integer. The wedge of length zero is the vacuum; it spans $V^{(r)}_0$. For $r=1$ these are the spaces $V_h$ of §13.1.

Put $c_0=\pi/\sqrt3$ (the constant $c$ of Lemma 13.2). The paper proves on pp. 74–75:

$$\dim V^{(r)}_e\le\exp\bigl(c_0\sqrt{re}\bigr).\tag{19.1}$$
Gap fill — derivation of (19.1), and the partition count behind "width is proportional to the square root of energy". filled here For $q\gt 0$ let $Z(q)=\prod_{m\ge0}\bigl(1+e^{-q(m+1/2)}\bigr)$. Expanding the product, each wedge of one species contributes $e^{-q\cdot\text{energy}}$, so $Z(q)=\sum_e\dim V^{(1)}_e\,e^{-qe}$. By fact (i) above, the generating function for $r$ species is $Z(q)^r$. Every term of a sum of nonnegative terms is at most the sum, so $\dim V^{(r)}_e\le e^{qe}Z(q)^r$.

The function $f(t)=\ln(1+e^{-t})$ is convex. For a convex function the value at the midpoint of an interval is at most its mean over the interval. Apply this to the interval $[qm,q(m+1)]$, whose midpoint is $q(m+\tfrac12)$, and sum over $m$: $$\ln Z(q)=\sum_{m\ge0}f\bigl(q(m+\tfrac12)\bigr)\le\frac1q\int_0^\infty f(t)\,dt=\frac{\pi^2}{12q}.$$ The integral is $\sum_{j\ge1}(-1)^{j+1}/j^2=\pi^2/12$ (expand $\ln(1+x)$ at $x=e^{-t}$ and integrate term by term). Hence $\ln\dim V^{(r)}_e\le qe+r\pi^2/(12q)$. The right side is least at $q=\pi\sqrt{r/(12e)}$, where both terms equal $\pi\sqrt{re/12}$; their sum is $\pi\sqrt{re/3}=c_0\sqrt{re}$. For $e=0$ the space is spanned by the vacuum and the bound is $1\le1$.

The count behind this: doubling the energies turns a one-species wedge of energy $h$ into a partition of $2h$ into distinct odd parts $2m+1$. Lemma 13.2 states $\dim V_h\sim\exp(c_0\sqrt h)/(2^{5/2}3^{1/4}h^{3/4})$, so the upper bound is sharp in the exponent. For $r$ species that share energy $e$ equally, each has energy $e/r$ and the log-dimensions add to $r\cdot c_0\sqrt{e/r}=c_0\sqrt{re}$. In bits, the width of a leg (the base-two logarithm of its dimension) satisfies $$w\le\frac{c_0}{\ln2}\sqrt{re}\approx2.617\sqrt{re},\qquad\text{so}\qquad w^2\le\frac{c_0^2}{(\ln 2)^2}\,r\,e .$$ Squared width is linear in energy, with slope proportional to the number of species.

The paper encodes a leg by the binary index of its basis state and pads unused codes with zero weights (p. 75). A leg of dimension $d$ thus becomes a bundle of $\lceil\log_2 d\rceil$ bits.

Definition (the sphere form $C_r$). p. 75 paper Let $z_1,\ldots,z_n\in K$ be distinct points, called marks or sites; they are points of the projective line over $K$, which the paper calls the sphere. The sphere form $C_r$ is a multilinear form on a tuple of wedges, one at each site. List the generators of all the wedges in site order. $C_r$ is the Pfaffian of this list with the following contractions. Two generators at a common site, or of different species, have contraction zero. For one species, the contraction of mode $m$ at site $i$ with mode $n$ at site $j$, in that order, is $$[x^my^n]\,\frac{1}{z_j+y-z_i-x}=\frac{(-1)^n\binom{m+n}{m}}{(z_j-z_i)^{m+n+1}}.\tag{13.1}$$ The Pfaffian of the empty list is one, and an odd list gives zero. The definition is over the integers and includes characteristic two by reduction.
Gap fill — the closed form in (13.1), and a generating-series way to read the kernel. filled here With $\Delta=z_j-z_i$, $\;\frac{1}{\Delta+y-x}=\sum_{n'\ge0}\frac{(x-y)^{n'}}{\Delta^{n'+1}}$. The coefficient of $x^my^n$ in $(x-y)^{m+n}$ is $(-1)^n\binom{m+n}{m}$. This gives the right side of (13.1).

It is convenient to attach to a site at position $p$ with local coordinate $x$ the formal series $\Psi(p+x)=\sum_{m\ge0}x^m\psi_m$. Then (13.1) says: the contraction of $\Psi(P_1)$ with $\Psi(P_2)$, in that order, is the kernel $1/(P_2-P_1)$, and the contraction of two modes is the corresponding coefficient. The kernel is antisymmetric in $P_1,P_2$, as a Pfaffian requires. The proof of Lemma 19.2 uses this reading.

The paper lists the properties of $C_r$ that it needs (p. 75); each one is inherited from the one-species form of §13.1.

  • Site exchange. Exchanging two adjacent sites with wedges of energies $e,d$ multiplies the form by $(-1)^{(2e)(2d)}$. This is (P2): a block of length $\equiv 2e$ passes a block of length $\equiv 2d$. When all energies are fixed, every such sign is a scalar that a table can absorb.
  • Poles. As a function of one position, with the others fixed, each value of the form is a rational function with poles only at the other positions; it is bounded at infinity. Each contraction (13.1) is a negative power of a difference of positions.
  • Species factorization. Contractions between different species vanish. After the generators are sorted by species, the Pfaffian is the product of the one-species Pfaffians, up to the sign of the sorting.
Background — Laurent expansion at a collision, and the two-site fusion maps $I_{e'}$. filled here Put two adjacent sites at $z$ and $z+u$, with wedges of energies $e$ and $m$, and expand the form in powers of $u$ at $u=0$. Sort the matchings by the pairs that join the two colliding sites (P3). A pair of modes $(n_1,n_2)$ between them contracts to a multiple of $u^{-(n_1+n_2+1)}$, that is, $u$ to the power minus the energy of the pair. The generators that are not paired inside the cluster are paired with the other sites; their kernels are regular at $u=0$. Taylor expansion of such a kernel in the offset replaces a generator $\psi_{s,d}$ at offset $\theta$ (here $\theta=0$ for the first site, $\theta=u$ for the second; the paper writes $a$ for the offset) by $$\psi_{s,d}\longmapsto\sum_{f\ge d}\binom fd \theta^{f-d}\psi_{s,f},\tag{13.3}$$ which is the identity $\Psi(z+\theta+x)=\sum_f(\theta+x)^f\psi_f$ read coefficient by coefficient. Each unit of mode raised carries one factor of $u$. Collecting powers of $u$: the coefficient of $u^{e'-e-m}$ is the same form with the two sites replaced by one site at $z$ of energy $e'$, applied to the vector $I_{e'}(v_1,v_2)$, where $v_1,v_2$ are the two colliding wedges and $$I_{e'}:V^{(r)}_e\otimes V^{(r)}_m\longrightarrow V^{(r)}_{e'}$$ is computed at offsets $0,1$: sum over the partial pairings between the two wedges, contract the pairs by (13.1) at positions $0,1$, translate the unmatched generators by (13.3), wedge them in order, and keep the part of energy $e'$. Only $e'\ge0$ with $e'-e-m\in\mathbb Z$ occur. This is Proposition 13.1 for a two-site cluster, applied species by species; the paper states the extension on p. 75. The exponent $e'-e-m$ is the order of the fusion. The rule used throughout §21 is $$\text{energy after fusion}=\text{sum of the two energies}+\text{order}.$$ Example (§13.2, p. 48): two generators $\psi_0,\psi_0$ at offsets $0,1$ have total energy $1$. Their internal contraction gives the vacuum, energy $0$, at order $u^{-1}$. If they stay unmatched, the first nonzero translated wedge is $\psi_0\wedge\psi_1$, energy $2$, at order $u^{+1}$.
Definition (seam, sewing, insertion). pp. 74–75 paper To join two spheres, choose one site on each and contract the two legs by equality on the wedge basis at an energy $e$: the joined form is $\sum_{a}C(\ldots,a,\ldots)\,C'(\ldots,a,\ldots)$, where $a$ runs over the wedge basis of $V^{(r)}_e$. The pair of sites is a seam, and $e$ is the energy of the seam. The paper calls this operation sewing. The paper uses the word insertion without a displayed definition; from its use on pp. 74 and 76, an insertion at a mark is a station whose ports include the leg of a sphere form at that mark (from §20 on: one leg on each of two spheres), together with ordinary bits. The sphere form itself is one more tensor of the network; a network of spheres sewn along seams, with an insertion table at every other mark, contracts to a number for each setting of the exposed bits.
Background — why sewing uses a basis equality, and why that creates a problem. filled here In a tensor network a wire is the equality pairing $\sum_a\varepsilon_a\otimes\varepsilon_a$ on a chosen basis. A seam is a wire of dimension $\dim V^{(r)}_e$ between two sphere forms. Fusion is an operation on the sphere form of one side: when a moving insertion with state $x$ collides with the seam site, the near sphere sees the vector $I_{e'}(a,x)$ in place of $a$. To continue the motion on the other sphere, this operator, which acts on the near index, has to be rewritten as an operator on the far index. For a general basis pairing, the transpose of a fusion map need not be a fusion map. Lemma 19.2 shows that here it is one, up to an explicit substitution $S_j$ applied to the moving state.
Lemma 19.2 (Transposition at a seam). p. 75 paper Let $a,b$ be basis states at energies $e,e'$, and $x\in V^{(r)}_m$. There are parity-preserving maps $S_j:V^{(r)}_m\to V^{(r)}_j$ for $j\le m$, independent of $a,b$, and a nonzero scalar $\alpha=\alpha(e,e',m)$ such that $$[I_{e'}(a,x)]_b=\alpha\sum_{j\le m}[I_e(b,S_jx)]_a .\tag{19.2}$$ The coefficient is taken in the indicated wedge basis.

Here $[v]_b$ is the coefficient of the basis state $b$ in the vector $v$, and "parity-preserving" means that $S_j$ maps wedges of length $d$ to combinations of wedges of length $d$. On the left, $x$ fuses with $a$ and the result is read at $b$. On the right, a modified $x$ fuses with $b$ and the result is read at $a$. The roles of input level $e$ and output level $e'$ are exchanged.

Step 1: a chart at infinity. paper Adjoin a square root $i$ of $-1$ to $k$; this is part of the "fixed finite extension" in Lemma 19.1. (In characteristic two, $i=1$.) Put a site at infinity with local coordinate $y$, so that its position is $1/y$, and multiply the kernel by $i/y$ for each generator at that site before extracting coefficients in $y$. For a finite position $U$ listed first, $$\frac iy\cdot\frac{1}{\tfrac1y-U}=\frac{i}{1-Uy}=i\sum_{n\ge0}U^ny^n .$$ With $U=x$ this says: mode $m$ at $0$ and mode $n$ at $\infty$ have contraction $i\,\delta_{mn}$.

Step 2: the two-point form at $0$ and $\infty$. paper Take basis states of lengths $d$ and $d'$ at $0$ and $\infty$. Contractions inside a site vanish, so $d=d'$ and each generator at $0$ pairs with the generator of the same species and mode at $\infty$. The two states are equal and the matching is unique. Its permutation sign is that of $(1,\ldots,d,1',\ldots,d')\to(1,1',2,2',\ldots)$, which is $(-1)^{d(d-1)/2}$. The value is $$\pi_d:=(-1)^{d(d-1)/2}\,i^d=\begin{cases}1,&d\ \text{even},\\ i,&d\ \text{odd},\end{cases}$$ and all other coefficients vanish. (Check: $d=1,2,3,4$ give $i,\ 1,\ i,\ 1$.) So the two-point form is the equality pairing on the wedge basis, up to the factor $\pi_d$.

Step 3: the three-point form at $0,1,\infty$ is a matrix coefficient of fusion. paper Evaluate the form at sites $0,1,\infty$, in this order, on $a,x,b$. Sort the matchings by their pairs between sites $0$ and $1$; these are the internal pairs in the definition of $I$, at offsets $0,1$. Each remaining generator pairs with a generator at $\infty$. By Step 1 with $U=\theta+x$, a generator $\psi_{s,d}$ at offset $\theta\in\{0,1\}$ and mode $n$ at infinity contract to $$[x^dy^n]\,\frac{i}{1-(\theta+x)y}=i\binom nd \theta^{\,n-d}.$$ This is the translation (13.3) followed by the contraction $i\,\delta$ of Step 1. By (P1) the remaining Pfaffian is the two-point form of Step 2 evaluated on the wedge of the translated unmatched generators and on $b$. Summing over the internal pairings gives $$C_{0,1,\infty}(a,x,b)=\pi_{|b|}\,[I_{e'}(a,x)]_b ,$$ where $|b|$ is the length of $b$ and $e'$ its energy; Step 2 selects the component of energy $e'$.

Step 4: inversion of the coordinate. paper Put $V=1/U$. The identity $$\frac{-i}{U_1}\cdot\frac{-i}{U_2}\cdot\frac{1}{\tfrac1{U_2}-\tfrac1{U_1}}=\frac{1}{U_2-U_1}$$ says that the kernel in the coordinate $U$ equals the kernel in the coordinate $V$ when each argument receives the factor $-i/U$. In the series notation, $\Psi(U)=(-i/U)\,\Psi'(1/U)$, where $\Psi'$ has the standard kernel in $V$. All contractions between different sites are preserved by this rewriting, so the three-point value is unchanged. The three sites transform as follows.

  • Old $\infty$: its chart series is $(i/y)\Psi(1/y)=(i/y)(-iy)\Psi'(y)=\Psi'(y)$. It becomes an ordinary site at $V=0$ with the same modes and factor one. It carries $b$.

Frozen stations: conclusion 1

The collectors become the frozen stations. The paper's argument (p. 84) has three charges for the arity of a collector: its two sphere legs, its external bits, and the additional thin wires.

Sphere legs. paper At a collector of weighted energy $e$, the two sphere legs have total width $O(T\sqrt{e/H})+O(M)$, by (20.2) and (22.1). §21.3 gives $\sum e=O(WH/T)$ over all collectors and $e=O(D^2H)$ at each one. filled here Hence $$\sum_{\rm collectors}\bigl(T\sqrt{e/H}\bigr)^2=\frac{T^2}{H}\sum e=O(WT),\qquad\max\,T\sqrt{e/H}=O(DT).$$ The unit $H$ cancels: the bounds depend only on the ratios $e/H$.

External bits. paper Every immobile station keeps its own external ports. Its external width is at most its input arity, so the squares of the external widths sum to at most $\sum_it_i^2\le WT$, and each is at most $100DT$.

Thin wires. paper All additional wires from motion and selectors together have total width $O(D^{-2\ell}T)$ per cell (§21.3). Even if all of them end at the same collector, their squared width is $O(D^{-4\ell}T^2)$ per cell. filled here Over at most $W/(DT)$ cells this is $O(WT\,D^{-4\ell-1})$.

Rounding. paper The additive terms $O(M)$ come from a number of chunks that is constant once $D$ is fixed. filled here Their squares sum to $O(M^2)\cdot O(W/(DT))$, which is at most $WT$ once $T^2\ge M^2$.

Combination. paper By $(x+y+z)^2\le3(x^2+y^2+z^2)$ the squared arities of the frozen stations sum to $O(WT)$, and the maximum arity is $O(DT)$. There are $O(W/(DT))$ collectors (§21.3), and the immobile stations are among them, in their original cells. Indices of the outer sum: conclusion 4

Conclusion 4 concerns the description of the terms and not the tensor identity. The paper argues it in prose on pp. 85–86. The walk below lists its assertions in order.

The index of a term. paper A bundle is encoded by its endpoint slots, its length, and its position in the ordered ports at its ends. No address of an individual exterior bit is needed, because a bundle is carried as an ordered list. The branching data at one occupied cell are: the initial chunk histograms (§20.2); the motion and fusion choices, namely positions, energies, interpolation points and orders (§21); and the final chunk splittings (this section). For fixed $D$ the number of entries is bounded, including the size of the alphabet of the histograms. Each integer ranges over $T^{O_D(1)}$, and a field point uses $O_D(\log T)$ bits because $[K:k]=O_D(\log T)$. This gives $O_D(\log(T+1))$ control bits per occupied cell. The private selector bits are wires inside the term and are not part of the index. Unused entries are set to zero, which makes the padding of an index unique.

The schedule. paper Use the supplied coloring of the $(4D^5+20)$-th power of the host; greedy coloring gives one with $O_D(1)$ colors for fixed $g$. Motions are started successively by color and station slot. Concurrent motions affect disjoint neighborhoods, since each has at most $D^5$ crossings. A complete padded schedule has length bounded in terms of $D,g$ only. filled here The power graph has maximum degree at most $g^{4D^5+21}$, so the greedy algorithm uses at most that many colors plus one. A move with at most $D^5$ crossings stays within host distance $D^5$ of its starting cell.

Labels. paper In each step the cells may send their local descriptions to their neighbors. A merger concatenates the descriptions of its two input tables and the operation parameters. A crossing adds the named interpolation, the fusion, and the new endpoints of its thin wire. The history of any resulting table draws on a number of these records that is bounded in terms of $D$. A label of length $O_D(A+\log(T+1))$ therefore specifies each entry as a function of its ports through the fixed dictionary. The dictionary may use arbitrary interpolation weights, restriction maps and internal contractions; generating labels requires no evaluation of entries.

No inspection of selector bits. paper Counts in a chunk histogram fix the energies and coded widths. Range and total checks admit every realizable histogram; the selector check, which is a table inside the network, gives zero whenever an admitted histogram is unrealizable. Interpolation may use a fixed ordered collection of all regular field points, with weights indexed by the local pole bounds. The scheduler only checks avoidance of marks, the ranges of the indices, and the rules of fusion and motion. Zero coefficients may be retained.

Cost. paper The checks and length estimates use arithmetic on integers or field coordinates of $O_D(\log(T+1))$ bits, including division with remainder and rounded square roots (the integer widths after (22.1)). The scheduler copies old labels as strings in fixed padded slots. At each cell the number of records and of operations on them is bounded independently of $T$. This gives $O_D\bigl(A+(1+\log T)^{O(1)}\bigr)$ bit operations and bits communicated per occupied cell, and proves conclusion 4.∎
